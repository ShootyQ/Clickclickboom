I said I would.

Then I tucked the blanket in where Via wanted it.

For a moment, neither of us said anything.

“You don't have to clean all of it,” Via said.

“I'll do what I can.”

Via looked at my face.

“You look tired.”

“I am a little tired.”

The blanket moved.

A little space appeared beside it.

“You can have some of mine if you want.”

I thanked Via.

Said I had a spot on the couch.

Via nodded.

Then held out a hand.

I held it for a moment.

“Good night.”

“Good night.”

I left the doors as Mom and Dad had asked.

Listened for a little while.

A quiet rustle.

A sleepy question that didn't quite make it into words.

Then the house began to settle.

I went downstairs with the folded paper.

The living room looked different from the one I'd arrived in.

The toy basket was empty.

Most of its contents seemed to have held a meeting across the rug.

The cushions were still a castle.

One of the castle walls had fallen into the hallway.

A microphone lay on a little plastic plate.

There was playdough on the table.

On a chair.

And, I discovered when I approached, in the couch.

Quite firmly in the couch.

A long green piece filled the gap between two cushions.

The bridge had reached its final destination.

Purple playdough had been pressed into a seam.

There was a tiny pretend cake beneath the lamp.

Bells had left the red pancake on its little plate.

For me.

I looked toward the kitchen.

Jelly weather remained on the wall.

The saucepan needed washing.

Three little bowls sat beside a fourth dish full of the dinner that had failed its interview.

The bathroom had become a separate problem upstairs.

I could remember enough of it without visiting.

I put away the food.

Set the plates safely in the kitchen.

Moved the toys out of the walking space.

Did what I could with the cloth.

The wall improved a little.

It was now more of a sunset than a weather event.

I picked up a cushion.

A toy car fell out.

I put it in the basket.

Picked up another cushion.

Three blocks and a small plastic spoon fell out.

I sat down on the couch.

Very slowly.

Something soft beneath me made a small squishing noise.

It wasn't entirely me.

I moved one cushion.

More playdough.

I moved another.

A sock.

The same sock Via had found when I arrived.

It had experienced quite an evening too.

I set it on the arm of the couch.

Then I lay down.

Not gracefully.

I spread along the cushions like somebody had poured out the last of a very tired jam jar.

My face ended up beside a small purple shape.

I didn't have the energy to identify it.

For a little while, I listened.

No footsteps.

No singing.

No requests involving treats.

I could hear the kitchen clock.

I hadn't known there was a kitchen clock.

I opened Via's folded paper.

Inside was a picture.

Three children.

One large red blob.

The blob was in the middle.

Each child had a hand against him.

There was a cushion beside his head.

A little plate near his feet.

Above us, in careful writing, Via had put:

THANK YOU FOR LOOKING AFTER US.

Underneath, in a different arrangement of letters, there was something Moore had helped with.

Via had written a small explanation beside it.

HE CAN KEEP THE CAR UNTIL NEXT TIME.

There was a little green mark in one corner.

Playdough, probably.

Bells had added a red heart.

It was enormous.

Much bigger than any of the people.

I looked at it for a long time.

Then I put the paper beside me where it wouldn't get flattened.

I was still there when Mom and Dad came home.

I heard the key.

The quiet sound of two people trying not to wake their children.

Then both parents stopped in the hallway.

There was a pause.

I watched their shoes through a gap in the cushions.

Dad moved one foot around the microphone.

Mom looked toward the kitchen wall.

Then at the couch.

Then at me.

“Everyone asleep?”

“Yes.”

“All okay?”

“Yes. Moore's elbow has a bear on it. Small scrape. He was feeling much better by bedtime.”

They nodded.

I told them the rest.

About the food.

The singing.

The playdough.

The bathroom artwork.

We went over the roof again.

Dad checked the window.

Mom listened while I explained what we'd done afterward.

Neither of them had forgotten that part.

Neither had I.

I didn't think I would.

Then Dad came back downstairs and picked a little green bit out of the couch.

He looked at it.

“Mac and cheese worked?”

“Beautifully.”

He nodded with the quiet satisfaction of a man whose advice had survived testing.

Mom took the little red pancake from the table.

“Did Bells make you dinner?”

“Yes.”

She looked at the tiny plate.

Then at the cushion the children had brought me.

A smile appeared.

Dad began gathering blocks from the rug.

Mom sat on the end of the couch where there was still room to be a person.

“How were they?”

I looked at the room.

The castle.

The jelly.

The microphone that had briefly made me famous across several gardens.

Then I looked at the picture beside me.

I told them about Bells bringing her toy to Moore.

About Via moving the things he'd fallen on.

About the little piece of treat Moore had saved for me.

The cushion.

The blanket.

The care they'd taken to make room for one another at story time.

Mom looked down at the picture.

Dad stopped picking up blocks for a moment.

“They're lovely kids,” I said.

I didn't have to think about it.

I was exhausted.

The house was a wreck.

But I'd spent the evening with them.

I'd seen the little things.

They were funny.

Curious.

Very determined about noodles.

And when someone needed a cuddle, they all seemed to know where to go.

Mom brushed a little dry playdough off my side.

“Would you do it again?”

I looked at the little car beside the drawing.

Until next time.

“Yes,” I said.

Dad smiled.

Mom smiled too.

I lifted my head a little.

“Not tomorrow.”

They laughed very quietly.

I lay back down.

Upstairs, somebody turned over in bed.

We all listened.

There was a small sleepy voice.

Bells.

“Good night, squishy.”

I called back softly.

“Good night.”

Then I closed my eyes for a moment.

One very tired blob.

Three very good kids.

I'd bring the car back next time.
