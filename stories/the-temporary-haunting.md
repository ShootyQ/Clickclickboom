# I Was Hired to Haunt a House

I once got a job haunting a house.

Temporary cover.

Three nights.

Possibility of becoming permanent, depending on whether anyone died.

I thought that meant me.

The woman at the agency said no.

Then she checked a box.

Which didn’t help.

The agency was above a dry cleaner’s.

You went up a narrow staircase, past a sign that said PLEASE DO NOT MATERIALISE IN THE STAIRWELL.

There was another sign underneath.

WE HAVE HAD THE HANDRAIL FIXED TWICE.

At the top was a waiting room.

Six chairs.

Five people.

One of the chairs was crying.

I sat somewhere else.

A man opposite me had an axe in his head.

Not freshly.

There was dust on the handle.

He caught me looking.

Said, “Work injury.”

I said I was sorry.

He said, “Don’t be. They finally gave me a chair.”

Before I could ask what he’d been doing before that, a woman opened a door.

“Next.”

Nobody moved.

She looked at the clipboard.

“The red one.”

I went in.

Her name was Denise.

She had glasses on a chain, a cardigan the colour of wet pavement, and a mug that said I WAS TOLD THERE WOULD BE REST.

She looked at me.

Then at the application.

Then at me again.

“You’ve put ‘flexible’ under availability.”

“Yes.”

“And under physical description.”

“Also yes.”

She made a note.

Asked whether I had any previous experience in the supernatural.

I mentioned the moon.

She put her pen down.

“Were you the one who called about the wolves?”

I said there had been several issues.

She nodded.

“We don’t handle celestial.”

Nobody does.

That’s how they get you.

She asked whether I could walk through walls.

I said I’d never managed it deliberately.

Could I turn invisible?

Not that anyone had mentioned.

Could I levitate?

I said I could fall quite slowly if the stairs were carpeted.

Denise looked at the application again.

There was a long pause.

Then she asked:

“Are you dead?”

I said no.

“As far as I know.”

She sighed.

Not at me.

At the situation.

Which was apparently worse than me.

It was October.

Everybody wanted haunting.

Nobody wanted to work weekends.

Two of her regulars were trapped in a mirror.

One had become religious and was refusing to go back into the cupboard.

And a poltergeist called Martin had put his back out throwing a wardrobe.

I asked whether ghosts had backs.

She said:

“They do when you need them.”

The position was at a house called Briar Lodge.

Large property.

One resident.

Established haunting.

The usual ghost had gone on leave.

“Can ghosts take leave?”

Denise looked at me over her glasses.

“Would you like to explain to him that he can’t?”

I said I wouldn’t.

She gave me a folder.

Inside were instructions, a key, and a photograph of a very tall house.

It had the sort of windows that looked as though they’d watched you arrive and weren’t impressed.

There were chimneys everywhere.

Not in a sensible row.

Just wherever someone had apparently thought the roof looked too cheerful.

A stone bird sat above the front door.

It might have been an eagle.

It might have been a very disappointed chicken.

The instructions said:

ARRIVE AFTER DUSK.

USE REAR ENTRANCE.

DO NOT DISTURB THE BLUE ROOM.

Underneath, in different handwriting:

SERIOUSLY.

I asked about the blue room.

Denise said:

“Every house has something.”

I said mine had a drawer that wouldn’t shut.

She said this was probably worse.

Then she gave me a sheet.

White.

Two eyeholes.

One of them was slightly higher than the other.

I put it on.

Denise examined me.

The sheet settled over my head, slid down my sides, and gathered on the floor.

I looked like someone had put a tablecloth over a dropped tomato.

She adjusted it.

Stepped back.

“No.”

I took it off.

She handed me a little black badge.

TEMPORARY APPARITION.

“Wear that.”

I pinned it on.

It slid down.

I held it.

She said that was fine.

Then she told me the most important rule.

“If anyone asks you to prove you’re a ghost, don’t.”

I asked why.

“Because then it becomes a conversation.”

She was right about that.

Most problems do.

I arrived at Briar Lodge just after seven.

There was a long gravel drive.

Wet trees leaned over it.

Somewhere in the dark, a branch scraped against another branch with the slow, deliberate sound of someone sharpening a complaint.

I’d brought a sandwich.

I was already looking forward to it.

The rear entrance had a bell.

I rang it.

Nothing happened.

I rang it again.

A voice from inside shouted:

“If you’re dead, you can wait.”

I waited.

Eventually, the door opened.

An elderly woman stood there holding a torch and half a piece of toast.

She was short.

Very upright.

White hair pinned so firmly it seemed to be helping hold the house together.

She looked down at me.

Then at my badge.

Then back at me.

“Oh, good,” she said.

“You’ve brought some colour.”

Her name was Mrs. Wetherby.

She shook the part of me I offered.

Very briskly.

Then she invited me in.

The kitchen was enormous.

There was a range cooker, a stone sink, seventeen cupboards, and one wooden chair that had been repaired so many times it was mostly agreement.

Copper pans hung above the table.

A clock ticked with the confidence of something that had never once been asked to hurry up.

Mrs. Wetherby put the toast down.

Asked whether I wanted tea.

I said yes.

She took out two cups.

Then paused.

“Can you?”

“I manage.”

“Good. I’m not getting a saucer dirty for appearances.”

She showed me where to put my things.

I put my sandwich in the fridge.

There was already a plate in there labelled GERALD.

I asked whether that was the usual ghost.

“No. That’s my son.”

“Does he live here?”

“Not unless I’m away.”

She closed the fridge.

“He likes the heating.”

Then she took me into the hall.

There were portraits.

Lots of them.

People in old clothes looking as though they’d just heard what the gardener was being paid.

A grandfather clock stood beside the staircase.

It was taller than some rooms I’d been in.

The pendulum moved behind the glass.

Slowly.

Like a large gold tongue disapproving of the time.

Mrs. Wetherby pointed at it.

“Don’t let that strike thirteen.”

I looked at her.

She looked at me.

Then she said:

“It takes the man three weeks to come out.”

I was beginning to understand the house.

She showed me the library.

The dining room.

A drawing room no one had drawn in.

A morning room apparently unsuitable after lunch.

There was also a small room containing only a chair and a bowl of walnuts.

I asked what that one was called.

“The little room.”

Sensible.

We went upstairs.

The staircase curved around a dark central space.

It had a thick runner, polished wooden rails, and a landing wide enough to hold a small wedding reception, provided nobody expected music or happiness.

Halfway up, Mrs. Wetherby stopped.

“Can you do this?”

She placed one foot on a stair.

Pressed.

The wood made a long, wounded creak.

She looked at me.

I moved onto the stair.

Pressed down.

Nothing.

I tried again.

There was a damp little squeak.

She considered it.

“Different,” she said.

“Still unpleasant.”

I thanked her.

At the landing, she pointed to a door.

Blue.

A very firm blue.

The kind used on doors people have already argued about.

“The blue room.”

I nodded.

“Don’t go in.”

“Right.”

“Don’t knock.”

“Fine.”

“And if someone knocks from inside, don’t get into it.”

I looked at the door.

There was a small brass keyhole.

Something on the other side made a faint clicking noise.

Mrs. Wetherby took my hand.

“Come along.”

She showed me the guest bedroom I’d be using during the day.

I asked whether ghosts slept.

“I don’t see why you should get out of it.”

Fair.

Then she gave me the evening schedule.

It was written on the back of an envelope.

Eight thirty: footsteps.

Nine fifteen: cold spot.

Ten: something upstairs.

Midnight: moaning.

I asked what sort of moaning.

She thought.

“Nothing personal.”

I could do that.

Probably.

At eight thirty, I started the footsteps.

I went up the stairs.

Then down.

Then up again.

Mrs. Wetherby was in the sitting room doing a crossword.

After a while, she called:

“Are you wearing slippers?”

I said no.

“Sounds as though you are.”

I tried putting more weight into it.

Squish.

Squish.

Squish.

There was a pause.

Then she appeared in the doorway.

Watched me climb three steps.

“Have you got shoes?”

“No.”

“Never mind.”

She went away.

Came back with two heavy boots.

Men’s boots.

Black.

Enormous.

The sort you’d wear to tell a field it was becoming a car park.

She put them on the stairs.

“There.”

I looked at them.

She looked at me.

“Use your initiative.”

So I carried them.

One in each little side.

And knocked them against the stairs as I went up.

Clomp.

Clomp.

Clomp.

It was quite convincing.

Until I dropped one.

It bounced down the staircase.

Hit the grandfather clock.

The clock struck four.

It was eight forty-two.

Mrs. Wetherby listened.

Then said:

“He won’t come out for that.”

At nine fifteen, I made the cold spot.

This involved standing behind the sitting-room door holding a bag of frozen peas.

It had been Denise’s suggestion.

On the telephone.

She’d sounded busy.

In the background, someone was shouting that the mirror had been like that when they arrived.

Mrs. Wetherby walked past me.

Stopped.

Held out a hand.

“Oh, yes.”

She moved it through the cold air.

“Very nice.”

Then she looked at the bag.

“Don’t let those thaw. They’re for Sunday.”

At ten, I did something upstairs.

The instructions hadn’t been more specific.

I moved a small table.

Then moved it back.

It made a scraping noise.

I waited.

Nothing.

I opened a cupboard.

Closed it.

Then I knocked a hairbrush off a dressing table.

From downstairs, Mrs. Wetherby called:

“Better.”

I was improving.

At midnight, I moaned.

I’d practiced.

Nothing too theatrical.

Just a low, distant:

“Ooooooooh.”

The house carried it beautifully.

It went along the corridor.

Down the stairs.

Into the hall.

Then Mrs. Wetherby shouted:

“What?”

I stopped.

She came to the bottom of the staircase.

“Did you want something?”

“No.”

“Then why are you calling me?”

“I’m moaning.”

“Oh.”

She thought.

“Do it away from the bathroom. It sounds as though you’ve had a fall.”

That was the first night.

At one, she made us cocoa.

We sat in the kitchen.

She asked how I’d found it.

I said there were things to improve.

She said there always were.

Then she took a folded piece of paper out of her cardigan.

Put it beside my cup.

“What’s this?”

“A few thoughts.”

I opened it.

Names.

Seven of them.

Beside each name was a date and a short explanation.

GERALD — SATURDAY — HOUSE.

MRS. PIKE — SUNDAY — JAM.

DEREK — MONDAY — LEAF BLOWER.

I looked up.

Mrs. Wetherby took a drink.

I looked down again.

There was a name near the bottom with three lines under it.

SIMON — WHENEVER YOU LIKE.

No explanation.

I asked about Simon.

“Knows what he did.”

I put the paper down.

“I’m not sure we’re supposed to haunt people outside the house.”

Mrs. Wetherby nodded.

“Good. Gerald’s coming here.”

Gerald was her son.

He arrived the next morning in a car so clean it seemed to resent the driveway.

He had a scarf.

A very soft scarf.

He wore it in the kitchen.

Which told me quite a lot.

He kissed his mother on the cheek without moving the scarf.

Then put a folder on the table.

“Mum. We should talk.”

Mrs. Wetherby looked at the folder.

“Should we?”

“The house.”

“What about it?”

“It’s a lot.”

She looked around.

“Yes. I’ve had it measured.”

Gerald smiled in a tired way.

A smile that suggested he’d practiced being patient in the car.

He said the heating bills were ridiculous.

The roof needed work.

There were too many stairs.

Mrs. Wetherby said she generally used one staircase at a time.

He opened the folder.

There were photographs of a flat.

Very white.

Very tidy.

The sofa had no opinion about anything.

There was a bowl on the table containing three wooden balls.

I felt sorry for whoever had to keep finding somewhere to put them.

Gerald said it was manageable.

Mrs. Wetherby said it looked washable.

He said there were facilities.

She asked whether one of them was her house.

He shut the folder.

Then he noticed me.

I was beside the fridge.

Trying to be a small decorative object.

He stared.

“What’s that?”

Mrs. Wetherby didn’t turn.

“The ghost.”

Gerald looked at me.

I looked at Gerald.

He said:

“It’s red.”

I said:

“Thank you.”

I don’t know why.

Sometimes that’s all you’ve got.

Gerald turned to his mother.

“Mum.”

She put the kettle on.

“It’s temporary. The usual one’s away.”

He looked back at me.

There was a long silence.

Then he said:

“Can it hear us?”

Mrs. Wetherby looked at him.

“Gerald. You’re in its workplace.”

He lowered his voice.

Which was somehow worse.

That evening, he stayed for dinner.

Mrs. Wetherby asked whether I could make an appearance.

Nothing elaborate.

Just enough to put him off discussing estate agents over pudding.

I said I’d try.

At eight thirty, I took the boots upstairs.

Clomp.

Clomp.

Clomp.

Gerald stopped talking.

Looked at the ceiling.

“What was that?”

Mrs. Wetherby cut her potato.

“Stairs.”

Clomp.

Clomp.

Then I dragged the chain.

There had been one in a cupboard.

Quite heavy.

With a label tied to it.

CURTAINS — WEST WINDOW.

I didn’t question it.

Gerald came into the hall.

Looked up.

I was behind the banister.

Holding the chain.

One boot had got stuck under me.

We looked at each other.

I remembered Denise’s advice.

Don’t prove anything.

So I said:

“Beware.”

Gerald blinked.

I waited.

He said:

“Of what?”

A good question.

I hadn’t prepared for a follow-up.

I looked around.

There was a low beam behind him.

I pointed.

“That.”

He turned.

Hit his head.

Not hard.

Enough.

Mrs. Wetherby appeared at the dining-room door.

Looked at Gerald rubbing his forehead.

Looked at me.

I lowered the chain.

She said:

“Excellent.”

Gerald left after pudding.

He took the folder.

He also took the little plate marked GERALD from the fridge.

It contained a sausage.

I hadn’t liked to ask.

Mrs. Wetherby watched him go.

Then she closed the door.

“Well,” she said.

“That’s the house off the menu for a fortnight.”

I said I wasn’t sure I’d done much.

She patted me.

“You warned him.”

The next day, Mrs. Pike came.

Sunday.

Jam.

I remembered the list.

Mrs. Pike lived two doors away, if you counted the field and a disused chapel as a door.

She brought a basket.

Inside were six jars of homemade marmalade.

Little squares of fabric tied around the lids.

Different fabrics.

One had chickens.

One had flowers.

One had a very small sailing boat looking much more comfortable than I was.

Mrs. Pike put them on the table.

“For the church sale.”

Mrs. Wetherby looked at the jars.

“Lovely.”

Mrs. Pike nodded.

“I thought you could manage the labels.”

“Could I?”

“You’ve such a steady hand.”

I looked at Mrs. Wetherby’s hand.

It was very steady.

Especially considering what she appeared to want to do with it.

Accepted tea.

Then explained that last year’s labels had been slightly crooked.

Not enough for most people to notice.

But people had noticed.

She wouldn’t name names.

It wouldn’t be kind.

Then she named three.

Mrs. Wetherby looked at me.

A small look.

Over the teapot.

I understood.

I moved behind Mrs. Pike’s chair.

Took a breath.

And whispered:

“Crooooooooked.”

I stopped.

Mrs. Wetherby poured tea.

Mrs. Pike turned around.

Not ideal.

Then I said:

“Labels.”

Quite quickly.

“Did that say labels?”

Mrs. Wetherby put the teapot down.

“What did?”

Mrs. Pike pointed.

I lowered myself behind the chair.

That was good.

I hadn’t meant to do it.

Said she could probably do the labels herself.

“You’ve got a gift.”

“No.”

“Hello?”

“Could someone let me out?”

“I’ve got a delivery.”
