# The Things I Hadn't Lost Yet

The first thing to come through my letterbox was a teaspoon.

No envelope.

Just a teaspoon, followed by a small piece of paper.

The spoon landed on the mat with a clink.

The paper said:

YOU’LL WANT THIS.

I stood over it for a while.

Then I checked the street.

Nobody.

I looked up and down the pavement.

A woman was walking a dog, but neither of them seemed to be carrying further cutlery.

I went back inside.

The spoon was one of mine.

I recognised the little bend in the handle.

I’d done that trying to get the last bit of something out of a jar.

I no longer remember what was in the jar.

Only that I had considered it worth changing the spoon.

I took it to the kitchen.

Opened the drawer.

My spoon was already there.

I looked at it.

Then at the one in my hand.

Then back into the drawer, in case the explanation was underneath the forks.

It wasn’t.

I put them side by side.

Same length.

Same bend.

Same tiny scratch near the bowl.

I had two of my spoon.

Not two matching spoons.

Two of the particular spoon I owned.

There is a difference.

I spent breakfast moving them around.

The one from the drawer on the left.

The one from the door on the right.

Then the other way round.

I hoped one might begin to look less like mine.

Neither cooperated.

Eventually, I made tea.

I used a fork to stir it.

It was a poor start to the day, but at least I knew where the fork had come from.

At eleven, I decided to clean behind the toaster.

This wasn’t connected.

I’d found a crumb in my tea and felt there ought to be consequences somewhere.

I moved the toaster.

Found several more crumbs.

Then a large one I thought might technically be toast.

I reached across the counter.

Caught the edge of the draining board.

And knocked a spoon behind the cooker.

I heard it fall.

One bright little clink.

Then silence.

I looked down the gap.

Darkness.

Dust.

Something grey I wasn’t prepared to identify without better company.

I tried reaching.

I stretched a little farther.

Then farther still.

At a certain point, I was reaching down a narrow space behind a cooker for an object I had recently acquired two of.

I withdrew.

Sat down at the table.

And looked at the remaining spoon.

YOU’LL WANT THIS.

I stopped being pleased about the spare.

The note was on the table beside it.

Plain white paper.

Block capitals.

No signature.

I turned it over.

Nothing.

I held it up to the window.

Still nothing, but better lit.

Then I examined the spoon again.

I was looking for a difference.

Anything.

A mark.

A label saying this sort of thing was normal.

There wasn’t one.

I put it in a mug and covered the mug with a plate.

I don’t know what I expected the plate to achieve.

I just wanted there to be something between us while I thought.

That afternoon, I rang Mrs Bell.

I asked whether anyone had delivered anything unusual to her house.

“How unusual?”

“Something through the letterbox.”

“Bills.”

“Other than bills.”

“A leaflet about a roof.”

“Was it your roof?”

“Presumably.”

I considered how to approach the spoon.

“Have you had any cutlery?”

There was a pause.

“For Christmas?”

“No. Recently.”

“Squish, are you all right?”

I looked at the plate on the mug.

“Yes.”

This was optimistic.

I asked whether she’d seen anyone near my front door.

“The postman.”

“Anyone else?”

“A man with a ladder.”

“When?”

“April.”

I thanked her.

She asked whether I’d like her to come round.

I almost said yes.

Then I pictured explaining the mug.

“I’m fine.”

“Have something to eat.”

People suggest that very quickly when they don’t want to join your problem.

I had a biscuit.

The spoon remained under the plate.

The next morning, a button arrived.

This time, there was an envelope.

No stamp.

No address.

Just my name, written neatly on the front.

Inside was a brown button and a note.

DON’T THROW IT AWAY.

I checked the spoon first.

Still there.

Then I looked at the button.

Four holes.

A little nick near the edge.

It belonged to my old coat.

The coat was hanging in the cupboard.

I went to it.

Counted the buttons.

All present.

I counted them again, touching each one.

Still all present.

I held the loose button beside the bottom one.

Same nick.

I sat down on the cupboard floor.

It seemed like somewhere reasonable to be.

For several minutes, I considered the possibility that I was being given spare parts.

That should have been comforting.

It wasn’t.

I didn’t like wondering how large the parts might eventually get.

I put the button in a saucer.

Then I put the saucer on a high shelf.

The spoon already had the mug.

I was running through crockery rather quickly.

At lunchtime, Mrs Bell called round.

She had brought soup.

Apparently, my voice on the telephone had suggested soup.

I thanked her.

She looked at the table.

“Why’s there a plate on that mug?”

“Keeping something clean.”

“What?”

“A spoon.”

She looked at the ordinary drawer where I kept ordinary spoons.

I let her.

I wasn’t taking the plate off.

She stayed while I ate.

It was good soup.

I used a tablespoon.

She noticed.

“Hungry?”

“Yes.”

That was the easiest answer I had given anyone in two days.

After she left, I decided to go out.

I needed air.

And bread.

Something ordinary and purchasable.

It had started to rain, so I got my old coat.

The bottom button caught on the cupboard handle.

I pulled.

Not hard.

Just enough.

The thread snapped.

The button bounced once.

Rolled across the floor.

And disappeared into a crack beneath the skirting board.

I watched the place where it had gone.

Then I looked up at the high shelf.

The saucer was still there.

So was the button.

I took off the coat.

The weather could have me.

I went out without it.

At the corner shop, I bought bread, milk, and a packet of biscuits.

The man behind the counter asked whether I needed a bag.

I said yes with a force that surprised both of us.

He gave me a large one.

I was grateful.

It was reassuring to receive an object from someone whose location I could currently confirm.

I came home.

Put the shopping away.

Made tea.

Then I sat facing the front door.

Nothing happened.

For an hour.

Then two.

By evening, I felt almost foolish.

Perhaps the spoon was a coincidence.

Perhaps the button was another coincidence.

Two coincidences.

Perfectly possible.

I wasn’t sure how either coincidence had found the letterbox, but I had been through a difficult couple of days.

I went to bed.

At half past seven the following morning, a shopping list came through the door.

Bread.

Milk.

Tea.

Apples.

Washing-up liquid.

My handwriting.

Even the little line under tea, which I always added after forgetting it once and becoming a person I didn’t enjoy.

At the bottom, in larger letters:

DON’T GET THE MELON.

I read it twice.

Then I looked at the shopping I’d bought the day before.

There had been no melon.

I hadn’t planned to buy a melon.

To be honest, I rarely planned to buy a melon.

It was the sort of fruit that demanded a separate intention.

You needed a knife.

A plate.

A willingness to continue eating melon after the interesting part.

I put the list on the table.

Then I checked the kitchen.

I don’t know why.

It didn’t seem likely that someone had secretly supplied a melon and wanted me to avoid noticing.

Still.

I checked.

None.

I spent the morning doing ordinary things.

Washing up.

Putting things away.

Looking at the door much too often to call it ordinary.

Around eleven, I remembered I needed washing-up liquid.

Then tea.

Then apples.

I took a fresh piece of paper.

Started a list.

Bread.

Milk.

Tea.

I drew the little line underneath.

Stopped.

Looked at the delivered list.

Then at mine.

Same paper.

Same writing.

Even the way the B leaned slightly towards the edge, as though it had remembered something in the next room.

I put down the pen.

There were two ways to think about this.

One was that somebody knew what I was going to do before I did it.

The other was that I ought to buy better paper so I could distinguish my own stationery during an incident.

I found that second thought easier.

I stayed with it for a while.

That night, I watched the door.

Properly.

I brought my chair into the hallway.

Made a flask of tea.

Put a packet of biscuits beside me.

Then I moved the biscuits to the other side, away from the letterbox.

I didn’t want the person responsible feeling welcome.

At ten, a car passed.

At eleven, a cat sat on the front wall and looked at me through the glass.

I looked back.

It washed a leg.

Whatever else was happening, the cat didn’t consider it urgent.

At midnight, I heard a small sound outside.

I sat up.

A scrape.

Then another.

I opened the door.

Very quickly.

The cat was sharpening its claws on my doormat.

It looked annoyed that I had interrupted.

“Sorry,” I said.

Then I remembered whose mat it was.

I shut the door without improving the situation.

By four, I had finished the tea.

By five, I had developed opinions about the comfort of my hallway.

At six, I heard birds.

At seven, I opened the door.

A blue notebook lay on the mat.

My notebook.

I stared at it.

I had watched that door all night.

Not continuously, perhaps.

There had been blinking.

And one period when I had closed my eyes to think more quietly.

But I would have heard a notebook.

I picked it up.

The cover had a bent corner.

There was a little pale mark where I’d peeled off a price sticker.

And a faint red thumbprint near the spine.

Mine.

I took it into the sitting room.

My notebook was on the little table beside my chair.

I put the delivered one next to it.

Same bent corner.

Same pale mark.

Same thumbprint.

I looked towards the kitchen.

I had another mug, if necessary.

I opened the notebook from the table.

Three pages used.

Ordinary things.

A telephone number.

A recipe I had written down too quickly to know how much onion.

A reminder to return a bowl.

I opened the delivered notebook.

Same number.

Same uncertain onion.

Same bowl.

Then more pages.

I sat down.

On the next page, in my handwriting:

RAIN AGAIN.

Below it:

MRS BELL WANTS THE BOWL.

I glanced at the bowl on the sideboard.

She probably did.

That wasn’t a prophecy.

It was a reasonable response to the length of time I’d had it.

The next page said:

BOUGHT PEARS. ONE BAD.

That annoyed me.

I hadn’t bought them yet and already there was a problem.

I turned another page.

Most of it was blank.

Then, halfway down:

HE ASKED WHETHER I WAS ALONE.

I SAID YES BEFORE CHECKING.

Underneath was a brown ring.

A tea stain.

I stopped reading.

I looked up.

The room was quiet.

Chair.

Table.

Window.

The familiar shapes of things that had always been there.

I looked at the doorway.

Then the gap beyond it.

I had never particularly worried about how much house I couldn’t see from one chair.

It was quite a lot.

I put down the notebook.

“Hello?”

Nothing.

I felt silly.

Then I felt silly for being relieved.

Then I felt something else and got up.

I checked the kitchen.

The cupboard under the stairs.

The little space behind the curtain where there was never anything except curtain.

I checked it anyway.

Upstairs, I paused outside the bathroom.

“Anyone in there?”

No answer.

I opened the door.

Empty.

I checked behind it.

Then the bath.

I wasn’t expecting someone in the bath.

But once you’ve asked a bathroom whether it’s occupied, you may as well finish.

The bedroom was empty too.

The wardrobe contained clothes.

I moved them.

Still clothes.

I did not investigate the laundry basket with much commitment.

Whatever was in there had already suffered.

I went downstairs.

Sat beside the notebook.

And read the sentence again.

I SAID YES BEFORE CHECKING.

I had checked now.

That ought to have helped.

Instead, I wondered whether checking early counted.

I didn’t like that thought.

I liked it even less once I realised I was trying to work out rules nobody had explained.

At nine, the doorbell rang.

I nearly became several smaller people.

Mrs Bell stood outside.

“Morning.”

“Morning.”

She looked at me.

“Did you sleep?”

“Some of me.”

She waited.

I tried to gather the rest.

“Could I have my bowl back?”

I looked towards the sitting room.

The notebook was open on the table.

RAIN AGAIN.

MRS BELL WANTS THE BOWL.

Behind her, the first drops began hitting the path.

“Oh,” I said.

“I don’t need it this second.”

“No. Of course.”

“I’ve got others.”

“Yes.”

“But this is the useful one.”

I fetched it.

Washed it again, though it was already clean.

Dried it very carefully.

I wanted one thing that morning to happen in an order I understood.

When I handed it over, she asked whether I was alone.

I looked at her.

She looked at me.

“Squish?”

I opened my mouth.

Then shut it.

The hallway seemed to get longer behind me.

“Why?”

She lifted a small covered dish.

“I’ve made too much crumble. Wondered how many portions.”

I looked at the dish.

Then at her.

“Just one.”

“All right.”

She passed it over.

I thanked her.

She walked home.

I shut the door.

Waited until I heard her gate.

Then I went straight to the notebook.

The sentence was exactly the same.

HE ASKED WHETHER I WAS ALONE.

He.

Not she.

I read it again.

I wasn’t certain whether that made it better.

I ate the crumble standing up.

It was difficult to enjoy, but I made an effort.

Mrs Bell had put custard in a separate little pot.

I wasn’t letting that go unappreciated.

By lunchtime, I had decided to change something.

Something small.

No dramatic choices.

I didn’t want to find out what happened if I dismantled a day.

Just a little alteration.

The notebook said I would buy pears.

The shopping list said not to get the melon.

Fine.

I would buy neither.

Then I remembered I needed apples.

I went to the shop.

Stood in front of the fruit.

Apples on the left.

Pears in the middle.

Melons on the right.

The melons looked normal.

That was almost offensive.

A piece of fruit had no right to sit there looking that harmless after contributing so much to my week.

I picked up an apple.

Put it in my basket.

Then another.

I was doing well.

Then I looked at the pears.

One had a little dark patch near the stem.

BOUGHT PEARS. ONE BAD.

I stared at it.

I could leave it.

Simply leave it.

Walk away with apples.

I stood there for quite a long time.

Eventually, the shopkeeper asked whether I needed help.

“No.”

“Anything wrong with that pear?”

“Potentially.”

He looked at it.

“Looks all right.”

“That’s what it wants.”

He watched me put one pear in the basket.

A different pear.

I made a point of selecting it from the far end.

I don’t know whom the point was for.

At the till, he asked whether I wanted anything else.

“No melon,” I said.

He glanced at the fruit display.

“All right.”

“Definitely no melon.”

“Fine.”

I paid.

He didn’t offer me a bag.

I think he wanted the conversation to end.

At home, I placed the pear on the counter.

Then I wrote in my ordinary notebook:

BOUGHT PEAR.

ONE.

SINGULAR.

I considered underlining it.

Decided against.

I was worried how much of my handwriting I might be creating by trying to disagree with it.

For the rest of the afternoon, nothing happened.

No letters.

No noises.

No unexpected people.

I began to relax.

A little.

Enough to make tea in an ordinary cup.

At half past five, something tapped against the front door.

Once.

Not a knock.

A light little tick.

I went into the hallway.

A small envelope lay beneath the letterbox.

Inside was a melon seed.

One.

Pale.

Slightly damp.

And a note.

THAT WASN’T WHAT WE MEANT.

I sat down on the bottom stair.

The seed rested in my hand.

I hadn’t bought a melon.

I hadn’t cut a melon.

As far as I knew, there was not a melon in the house.

And yet somebody had provided a part.

I looked up at the letterbox.

“What did you mean?”

Nothing.

I waited.

Then I felt ridiculous asking the front door a follow-up question.

I stayed there anyway.

After a while, I said, “Could you be more specific?”

The house was quiet.

Outside, someone put a bin away.

An ordinary clatter.

I found it comforting until I realised I was listening to my neighbour’s rubbish as proof that the world still worked.

I took the seed into the kitchen.

Put it in a small dish.

The button remained on the shelf.

The spoon remained in the mug.

The objects had begun to look like a very disappointing museum.

I checked the pear.

Soft at the bottom.

I turned it over.

A dark patch had appeared.

I hadn’t won anything.

That evening, I read the delivered notebook from beginning to end.

Slowly.

Most of the later pages were blank.

After the sentence about being alone, there was only one more entry.

DON’T LET HIM FIND THE OTHER ONE.

No mention of what.

Or who.

Or which one was already found.

I turned the page.

Blank.

The next.

Blank.

I held the last page up to the lamp.

Nothing.

I was beginning to dislike paper.

It had a great deal of space in which to be unhelpful.

I put both notebooks in a drawer.

Then I took them out.

If something was going to be added, I wanted to know.

I left them side by side on the table.

I made sure the tea was nowhere near either.

Then I sat down.

At a quarter past eight, I heard a sound upstairs.

A quiet click.

Like a handle being tried.

I looked at the ceiling.

The bedroom door was open.

I knew it was open because I had left every upstairs door open after checking.

This had seemed sensible earlier.

Now I wished I had at least one fewer place to imagine.

The click came again.

Then a faint scrape.

I stood.

Something slipped through the letterbox.

I heard it hit the mat.

A soft, heavy little thud.

I stayed where I was.

For a moment, I wanted very badly to be the sort of person who could say, “I’ll look at that tomorrow.”

I wasn’t.

Not that evening.

On the mat was my tea towel.

The yellow one.

Folded around something.

I went to the kitchen.

The yellow tea towel hung beside the sink.

I touched it.

Then I went back to the hall.

Unwrapped the other one.

Inside was a key.

Long.

Dark.

Warm.

Not warm from the room.

Warm as though someone had just let go of it.

I held it by the edge.

There was a small red mark near the teeth.

I looked at my little hand.

Then at the key.

No note this time.

For once, I would have appreciated the paper.

Upstairs, something moved.

A footstep, perhaps.

Or a chair being adjusted.

I almost called Mrs Bell.

My hand was halfway towards the telephone when I imagined telling her I’d received a warm key in a duplicate tea towel.

She would come round.

Of course she would.

With soup.

She would stand in the hall.

Then we would both have to hear whatever happened next.

I put down the telephone.

I wasn’t being brave.

I just didn’t want to invite her into it before I knew where it was.

I climbed the stairs.

Slowly.

The key sat warm in my hand.

At the top, the bedroom door was still open.

So was the bathroom.

The landing light shone on the wall between them.

There had always been a narrow stretch of wall there.

Plain wallpaper.

A little crooked seam I had been meaning to flatten.

I knew that seam.

I’d pressed it with my thumb more than once while thinking I should fetch some glue.

Now there was a door.

Narrow.

Painted cream.

A small brass handle.

The wallpaper seam ended against its frame.

I stopped on the top step.

Behind that wall was the side of the house.

Beyond that was outside.

There wasn’t room.

I knew there wasn’t room.

I lived there.

I was familiar with the amount of indoors I owned.

A thin strip of light showed underneath.

I looked towards the bathroom.

Still empty.

Towards the bedroom.

Still mine.

Then back at the door.

Something inside scraped gently across the floor.

A chair leg.

I knew that sound rather well.

I looked down at the key.

It had cooled a little.

The red mark remained.

I took one small step onto the landing.

The floorboard creaked.

The sound inside stopped.

I stopped too.

For several seconds, neither of us did anything.

Then someone behind the door cleared their throat.

A small, familiar sound.

I felt my little hand tighten around the key.

A voice said:

“Before you open it, have you had anything to eat?”

It was my voice.

Not similar.

Not somebody trying.

Mine.

The voice I heard when I forgot something.

The voice that apologised to cupboards.

The voice I had been using all week to ask questions nobody answered.

I looked at the handle.

Then at the warm place the key had left in my hand.

I hadn’t.

But I didn’t like him knowing that.
