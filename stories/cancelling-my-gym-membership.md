# I Tried to Cancel My Gym Membership

I joined a gym because it was raining.

That is the whole reason.

People always expect a better one.

They ask whether I wanted to improve my health or build confidence.

I wanted to be on the other side of a door.

There was a woman outside handing out leaflets, and she said I could come in for a free tour.

I said, “Can I be dry during it?”

She said absolutely.

Twenty minutes later, I had a membership, a water bottle, and an appointment with a man who wanted to measure my waist.

I asked where he intended to start.

He said we’d work that out together.

I didn’t go back.

For six months, the gym took money out of my account.

Every month, I thought, I should sort that out.

Then I wouldn’t.

It was the most consistent fitness routine I’ve ever maintained.

Eventually, I went in to cancel.

I chose a sunny day.

I wasn’t falling for the weather again.

The gym was called PEAK POTENTIAL.

Underneath, in smaller letters, it said:

BECOME WHO YOU WERE MEANT TO BE.

I hoped I was meant to be someone with twelve pounds more a month.

The receptionist wore a headset and was eating yoghurt with an expression that suggested she hadn’t personally chosen the yoghurt.

“Hi,” I said. “I’d like to cancel my membership.”

“Of course!”

Wonderful.

Lovely.

A short story, for once.

“Membership number?”

I gave it to her.

She typed.

“Reason for leaving?”

“I don’t come here.”

She selected something.

“Is that lack of time or lack of motivation?”

“Presence.”

Her finger hovered over the screen.

“I’ll put motivation.”

“I’m quite motivated to leave.”

She smiled professionally, which is different from smiling because somebody has pleased you.

“Have you considered freezing your membership?”

“I’d prefer it dead.”

She typed again.

“Any injuries?”

“None I brought with me.”

Another smile.

Another click.

Then she looked towards a door behind her.

“I’ll just get our member success specialist.”

“That sounds like someone who helps me succeed.”

“He’s very good.”

I should have asked at what.

She pressed a button on her headset.

“Clive? Cancellation.”

There was a pause.

Her face changed.

“No, I haven’t let him out.”

I looked at the door.

Then at her.

She put a hand over the microphone.

“Through there, please.”

The office was small.

The desk was normal-sized.

The chair behind it was normal-sized.

The hamster sitting on the desk was not.

He was tiny.

He wore a black polo shirt with PEAK POTENTIAL embroidered over his heart in lettering so small it looked like a printer had sneezed.

A whistle hung around his neck.

It was larger than his head.

He stood beside a laptop and a mug that said NO EXCUSES.

I could have lived comfortably in the mug.

“Squish!”

He said my name like we’d survived something together.

“Clive,” he said, extending one little paw.

I shook it as gently as I could.

He gripped surprisingly hard.

“Good squeeze. Something to build on.”

“I’ve come to cancel.”

“Love a clear goal.”

He indicated the chair.

I climbed into it.

Clive watched me settle.

His eyes narrowed thoughtfully.

“Mobility,” he murmured.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

He took out a pen.

The pen was normal-sized too.

He had to hold it with both paws.

“Talk me through where you’re at.”

“Your office.”

“And where would you like to be?”

“Outside your office, no longer paying you.”

“Excellent. So we’ve identified a gap.”

I looked at the door.

It did seem a little farther away than before.

Clive opened my file.

“You’ve been with us six months.”

“Yes.”

“First of all, congratulations.”

“On what?”

“Commitment.”

“I haven’t attended.”

“But you haven’t quit.”

“That’s what I’m trying to—”

“Exactly. You’ve come in. That’s step one.”

I sat quietly.

I was beginning to suspect step two would be expensive.

Clive looked at the file.

“I can see you’ve had no recorded visits.”

“Correct.”

“No classes.”

“Correct.”

“No personal training.”

“Yes.”

“No induction.”

“I had a tour.”

He looked up.

“How did that feel?”

“Dry.”

He wrote something down.

I leaned forward.

He tilted the paper away.

“What did you write?”

“Responds well to environment.”

He put the pen down and clasped his paws.

“Squish, what if I told you we could find an exercise you genuinely enjoy?”

“I’d believe you. I’d still like to cancel.”

“What do you enjoy doing?”

“Sitting somewhere I chose.”

“Recovery-focused. Great.”

“And I don’t have muscles.”

Clive smiled.

“Not yet.”

I gave him a moment.

“No,” I said. “Not in the sense you mean.”

He nodded slowly.

I could see him deciding to admire my honesty.

It was infuriating.

“Tell you what,” he said. “Before we make any decisions, let’s get you moving.”

“I’ve made the decision. That’s what brought me in.”

“Exactly. Movement.”

He hopped off the desk.

There was a small staircase down the side that I hadn’t noticed.

Seven little carpeted steps.

Halfway down, he stopped, put his paws on his hips, and drew a deep breath.

“Leg day yesterday.”

I waited.

“Good sore,” he said.

“I didn’t ask.”

“No. But you noticed.”

He finished descending.

Then he led me out onto the gym floor.

I followed because I am polite in ways that have repeatedly harmed me.

The gym was full of people lifting things, lowering things, and briefly sitting beside things with their heads in their hands.

A man was pushing a large weighted sled across a strip of carpet.

It looked like a difficult way to move something that could have had wheels.

Clive waved at him.

“Nice work, Darren!”

Darren did not seem to be enjoying his nice work.

He made a noise into his knees.

Clive turned to me.

“That could be you.”

“I’m going to hold on to the word ‘could.’”

We started with stretching.

Clive put down a mat.

“Reach towards your toes.”

I looked at him.

He looked at me.

“Where your toes would be.”

“I’ve had this conversation before.”

“Just reach down.”

I reached down.

Then farther.

Then I went underneath myself and came back out on the other side.

Clive’s mouth fell open.

I put my little arm back.

“Was that enough?”

He took out his phone.

“No.”

He stepped back.

“Do it again.”

“Why?”

“I need to show Martin.”

“Does Martin cancel memberships?”

“Martin runs yoga.”

I folded my arms.

Clive put the phone away.

“Right. Strength.”

We went to the dumbbells.

He selected a small pink one.

I appreciated this.

It looked manageable.

“Grip that.”

I gripped it.

“Now lift.”

I pulled.

My arm stretched.

The dumbbell remained where it was.

I pulled harder.

My arm grew longer.

Clive watched with interest.

“Keep your elbow tucked.”

“Could you point to it?”

He walked round me.

I let go.

My arm slapped back against my side.

A woman on an exercise bike looked over.

I smiled to reassure her.

This is a habit of mine.

Something dreadful happens, and I reassure the witnesses.

Clive was still circling.

“Resistance bands,” he said.

“I’d prefer a cancellation form.”

“I’m thinking.”

“So am I, but mine has an ending.”

He brought a length of elastic from a rack.

“Let’s try a partner exercise.”

He handed me one end.

Then he walked backwards holding the other.

His little trainers squeaked.

“Maintain tension.”

I maintained tension.

He leaned back.

The band stretched.

He leaned farther.

His feet slid half an inch.

“Good,” he said.

The band stretched again.

So did I.

Now there were two elastic things involved, and only one of them was being paid to understand the situation.

“Clive.”

“Hold.”

His heels left the floor.

“Clive.”

“Core tight.”

“You’re airborne.”

He looked down.

“Oh.”

He let go.

I caught him.

Fortunately.

He landed in me up to his armpits.

For a second, we both stayed still.

His little paws rested on my surface.

He looked like a furious man who had discovered the hotel hot tub was lukewarm.

“Thank you,” he said.

“You’re welcome.”

A pause.

“Good reflexes.”

“Please don’t turn the rescue into a sales point.”

He climbed out.

Straightened his shirt.

Picked a piece of me off his whistle.

“Cardio,” he said.

I should have left.

There were doors.

I could see them.

One had a large green sign above it showing a person running away.

In hindsight, that person had the right idea.

Clive took me to a treadmill.

He hopped onto the side rail.

“Walking pace.”

“I don’t walk.”

“Travelling pace.”

He pressed START.

The belt began to move.

I stayed where I was.

For about half a second.

Then the belt carried me backwards.

“Oh,” said Clive.

I reached forward.

My front stayed near him.

My back continued towards the wall.

“Keep moving,” he said.

“I’m moving in sections.”

“Bring your lower body forward.”

“Which part do you think that is?”

He pressed a button.

The machine got faster.

“Wrong button,” he said.

It was nice to hear him take ownership of something.

I stretched along the belt.

My bow tie caught briefly on the cup holder.

Then it let go.

I came off the back in a soft roll and stopped against a bin.

Clive hit the red button.

The belt stopped.

He hurried over.

“You all right?”

I looked up at him from beside the bin.

“I have achieved quite a lot of leaving, but none of the useful kind.”

He offered me water.

I took it.

The bottle was free, apparently.

This made it my second free water bottle.

At this rate, each one had cost me thirty-six pounds.

Clive sat beside me.

For the first time, he didn’t have anything ready to say.

We watched a man across the room perform a very small movement with a very large weight.

He grunted.

Put it down.

Looked at himself in the mirror.

Then nodded, as though he had just won an argument nobody else had heard.

“Do you enjoy this?” I asked Clive.

“The gym?”

“Yes.”

He looked surprised.

“Of course.”

“All of it?”

He hesitated.

“Most of it.”

“The treadmill?”

“Not particularly.”

“The weights?”

“Some.”

“The man dragging furniture?”

“The sled.”

“Would you do that if he wasn’t watching?”

Clive glanced at a large man near the desk.

Martin, perhaps.

The man was eating chicken from a plastic box.

He looked like he owned seven identical plastic boxes.

Clive lowered his voice.

“No.”

“Then why do you tell me I’ll love it?”

He rubbed one paw over his face.

“Because sometimes people do.”

That was fair.

Annoyingly fair.

Before I could reply, music exploded from the studio.

A woman shouted, “WHO’S READY?”

Several people shouted back.

None of them sounded completely ready.

Clive stood up.

“Actually,” he said, “there is one class—”

“No.”

“You haven’t heard—”

“No.”

“Low impact.”

“I’ve already hit a bin.”

He nodded.

“Fair.”

He was learning.

Then someone came out of the studio and called, “We’re one short!”

Clive looked at me.

I looked at Clive.

“No.”

He took a breath.

I raised a little arm.

“Clive. Look at me.”

He did.

“I am not a gap you need to fill.”

He considered that.

Then he called, “We’re cancelling a membership, Sharon!”

It was the nicest thing anyone had said to me all day.

We went back towards his office.

That should have been the end.

Unfortunately, Darren was still pushing the sled.

He had reached the far end of the carpet.

Now he was coming back.

His face was a remarkable colour.

Clive stepped aside.

I stepped aside.

A little puddle of me did not.

I hadn’t realised I’d left it.

Sometimes, after a stressful experience, you don’t quite gather all your thoughts.

Or your bottom.

The front runner of the sled touched the puddle.

Darren pushed.

The sled moved easily.

Much more easily than Darren expected.

His expression brightened.

“Oh!” he said.

He pushed harder.

The sled accelerated.

Darren did not.

For a brief moment, his arms were straight out in front of him and his face contained the pure joy of a man who believed he had become stronger during the walk back.

Then the sled left the carpet.

“Darren,” said Clive.

It continued onto the polished floor.

“Darren.”

Darren ran after it.

“I’ve got it!”

He had not got it.

I recognised the phrase.

The sled headed towards the reception desk.

The receptionist looked up from her yoghurt.

Her mouth moved.

I couldn’t hear what she said over the music, but it wasn’t “Peak Potential.”

Clive blew his whistle.

It made almost no noise.

He shook it.

Blew again.

There was a tiny squeak.

“It’s decorative,” he said.

“Of course it is.”

I moved after the sled.

Fast.

Not because I had discovered a love of cardio.

Because the receptionist had nowhere to go and a spoon in her mouth.

I caught the rear bar.

My arms stretched.

My body flattened.

The sled slowed.

Behind me, Clive grabbed hold of me.

This made no practical difference, but I appreciated the contribution.

Darren caught up and seized Clive.

Now there were four of us involved.

The sled.

Me.

Clive.

Darren.

Darren leaned back.

Clive made a small compressed noise.

“I’m the middle,” he said.

The sled stopped.

One inch from reception.

The receptionist took the spoon out of her mouth.

She looked at Darren.

Then at me.

Then at Clive, whose feet were dangling between us.

“Cancellation?” she asked.

“Yes,” I said.

She nodded.

“I’ll get the form.”

Clive brushed himself down.

Darren was breathing very hard.

He looked at the sled.

Then at the long trail across the floor.

“What weight was on that?”

Clive glanced at him.

“Darren.”

“No, because I moved it quite fast.”

“Darren.”

“I’m just saying. For the record.”

The receptionist handed me a form.

A single sheet.

It had been under the desk the entire time.

I looked at it.

Then at her.

She looked at Clive.

Clive looked at the floor.

“Right,” I said.

We returned to his office.

He climbed the little stairs more slowly this time.

I sat.

He pushed the form towards me.

Name.

Membership number.

Reason for leaving.

I wrote:

I DON’T WANT TO COME HERE.

Clive read it.

Then he turned the form over.

There were more boxes.

“Would you recommend Peak Potential to a friend?”

I looked at him.

“Depends what they’d done.”

He made a little sound.

Not a coaching sound.

An actual laugh.

He tried to hide it by picking up the pen.

It took both paws again.

That spoiled the effect.

He entered something on the laptop.

Clicked.

Waited.

His whiskers twitched.

“What?” I asked.

“Nothing.”

He clicked again.

“Clive.”

“There’s a screen.”

“Can I see it?”

He turned the laptop.

WOULD THIS MEMBER BENEFIT FROM A FINAL MOTIVATIONAL CONVERSATION?

Underneath were two buttons.

YES.

SCHEDULE FOR LATER.

I stared.

Clive stared.

“Where’s no?” I asked.

He scrolled.

We found it at the bottom.

Very small.

Under a picture of a mountain.

Clive clicked it.

Another box appeared.

ARE YOU SURE?

“Yes,” we said together.

He clicked again.

A third box.

WHAT PREVENTED YOU FROM SAVING THIS MEMBER?

Clive sat back.

For a while, he didn’t move.

Then he looked at me.

“What should I put?”

“The member didn’t want a gym membership.”

He typed it.

Slowly.

With two fingers.

Then he added a full stop.

It seemed to cost him something.

He pressed SUBMIT.

The printer made a noise.

A piece of paper emerged.

Clive went down the stairs.

Retrieved it.

Came back up.

Placed it in front of me.

“Cancelled.”

I read it.

Properly.

Twice.

There was no appointment.

No pause.

No exciting opportunity to return stronger.

Just cancelled.

I could have kissed him.

I didn’t.

He’d had enough of being unexpectedly inside me for one afternoon.

“Thank you,” I said.

“You’re welcome.”

He sat on the edge of the desk.

Without his notebook or his pen, he looked much smaller.

“Sorry about the treadmill.”

“Thank you.”

“And the band.”

“Yes.”

“And the dumbbell.”

“That one mostly hurt my pride.”

He nodded.

I got out of the chair.

At the door, I stopped.

“Would you like to get a sandwich?”

Clive looked up.

“Now?”

“If you’ve got a break.”

He glanced at the clock.

Then towards the chicken man.

“What kind of sandwich?”

“I haven’t planned it that far.”

“How much protein?”

“Clive.”

He rubbed his face.

“Sorry.”

He took off the whistle.

Set it beside the mug.

Then he followed me out.

At reception, the woman handed him a clipboard.

“Two cancellations while you were in there.”

Clive looked at it.

“Forms are in the drawer,” he said.

She blinked.

Then she opened the drawer.

I waited while he climbed down from the desk.

Outside, the sun was still shining.

We went to a café.

Clive ordered cheese and pickle.

Then he looked at me.

“Is that all right?”

“It’s your sandwich.”

He seemed pleased with this arrangement.

We sat by the window.

For several minutes, we ate without discussing what the food would do for us.

Then Clive said, “You know, you did stop the sled.”

I put my sandwich down.

He raised both paws.

“No. I’m not going to say it.”

“Good.”

A pause.

“You were quite quick, though.”

“Clive.”

He took a large bite.

Chewed.

Looked out of the window.

I could practically hear him not offering me a programme.

I respected the effort.

A week later, I passed the gym.

Clive was outside eating a pastry.

He waved.

I waved back.

“Still cancelled?” he called.

“Still cancelled.”

“Good!”

He looked genuinely pleased.

Then he glanced at the sky.

Dark clouds were gathering.

He looked back at me.

“Looks like rain.”

I kept moving.
