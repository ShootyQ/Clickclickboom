# I Found a Dragon in the Supermarket

I went to the supermarket for three things.

Milk.

Bread.

Something for dinner.

I hadn't decided what the third thing would be.

I thought the supermarket might have suggestions.

It had quite a large one.

I found him beside the customer-service desk.

Sitting inside a shopping basket.

Little wings tucked neatly against his back.

Tail through the handle.

Quietly eating a receipt.

At first, I thought he was a toy.

A very good toy.

Green scales.

A round little belly.

Four feet that looked as though they'd been supplied for a larger animal.

There were two small horns above his eyes, and a soft fold behind each one that moved when someone passed.

Then he sneezed.

A tiny puff of smoke came out.

The receipt curled at the end.

He looked down at it.

Looked at me.

Then very carefully tucked the receipt beneath himself.

I understood that expression.

I'd worn it beside several things I wasn't meant to be doing.

“Hello.”

His little ear folds lifted.

He made a questioning noise.

Not a roar.

More the sound a kettle might make if it hoped you'd come and sit with it.

I crouched beside the basket.

He was about the size of a loaf of bread.

A generously filled loaf.

The sort you choose because you're having soup and want to feel the soup has company.

“Are you lost?”

He stood up.

Put two little feet on the side of the basket.

Then climbed directly into my arms.

I hadn't offered my arms.

But they were there.

He seemed to consider that sufficient.

He settled against me and let out an enormous relieved sigh.

It was a very large sigh for the amount of dragon involved.

His tail curled around my side.

He put his chin down.

Apparently we'd sorted it out.

I looked around for someone whose shopping had suddenly become less green.

Nobody was looking back.

The woman at customer service was on the telephone.

She held one finger up when she saw me.

I waited.

The dragon waited too.

He did it with his eyes shut.

I felt we'd arrived at different conclusions about my responsibilities.

There was a collar around his neck.

A little brass tag hung from it.

I turned the tag over.

PIPPIN.

Underneath, in slightly larger letters:

DO NOT GIVE HIM CHEESE.

Then, beneath that:

YES, EVEN A LITTLE.

I read it twice.

Pippin opened one eye.

I looked at him.

He closed the eye again.

There was a number on the back of the tag.

I tried to read it.

He tried to eat it.

We had a small disagreement about which of us needed the back of the tag more.

I won by moving it beyond his nose.

He stretched his neck.

It was a surprisingly long neck when he wanted somebody else's property.

“It's yours,” I said. “I'm trying to get you home.”

He licked the tag.

I took that as encouragement.

The woman put down the telephone.

Her name badge said DENISE.

She looked at Pippin.

Then at the basket.

Then back at Pippin.

“Is he yours?”

“No.”

She nodded very slowly.

“I was hoping he was yours.”

“So was he, I think.”

Pippin's little tail tightened around me.

Denise came around the desk.

She'd found the basket there a few minutes earlier.

Nobody had handed him in.

He'd just appeared beside the lost umbrellas.

She'd been hoping for a quiet break.

Instead, she'd dealt with a returned melon, a missing handbag, and a man who wanted to know why an onion was smaller than last week's onion.

“What did you tell him?”

“That they aren't all the same onion.”

I thought that was fair.

He hadn't.

He'd asked for the manager.

The manager was currently discussing onions in the vegetable aisle.

Denise tried the number on the tag.

It rang.

Then went to a message.

She left the details.

Pippin listened to the telephone with his head tilted.

When she put it down, he looked at the receiver.

Then at me.

I think he'd recognized the voice.

He pressed closer.

I gave him a little rub beneath his chin.

His eyes went half shut.

That seemed to be the correct place.

Denise made an announcement.

“Would the owner of a small green dragon please come to customer service.”

She paused.

“His name is Pippin.”

Pippin lifted his head when he heard his name come from the ceiling.

He looked up.

Around.

Then behind my shoulder.

He seemed impressed by how widely I was known.

Three people arrived.

None of them owned him.

One had heard dragon.

One had heard Pippin and thought it might be a missing child.

He wasn't holding anything.
