# Dinner at Basil's

I once went on a very nice date.

The woman was lovely.

The bread was a little forward.

Her name was Edie.

We'd spoken a few times before.

Once in a bakery.

Then again outside the bakery, when we'd both pretended we hadn't come back for another bun.

She had a quick smile and a small green coat with one button that didn't match the others.

I'd noticed the button.

I don't know why.

I liked that she hadn't replaced all the other buttons to make the one wrong button feel better.

When I asked her to dinner, she said yes.

I asked whether Friday would suit.

She said yes again.

I went home and spent a surprising amount of time thinking about Friday.

I wanted to choose somewhere nice.

Somewhere with proper chairs.

Somewhere the menu didn't arrive stuck to a bottle of vinegar.

I chose Basil's.

The website called it an intimate dining experience.

There was a photograph of a candle beside a pear.

The pear looked expensive.

I made a reservation.

Two guests.

Seven thirty.

No allergies.

The form also asked whether either guest had a fear of being recognized by a vegetable.

I put no.

I couldn't speak for Edie.

But it seemed unlikely to come up.

On Friday, I arrived early.

I'd brought flowers.

Nothing enormous.

Just a little bunch with yellow bits in it.

I don't know much about flowers.

The woman in the shop had asked what I wanted them to say.

I said hello, probably.

She'd nodded and wrapped these.

I hoped they'd remember.

Basil's stood on a quiet street between a clock repairer and a shop that only seemed to sell very long candles.

The restaurant window was dark green.

Its name appeared in small gold letters.

Underneath, it said: PLEASE RING ONCE.

I rang once.

Somebody inside said, “Splendid.”

The door opened.

A rabbit stood in front of me.

Quite a tall rabbit.

He wore a bottle-green waistcoat, polished shoes, and a white shirt with one cuff slightly damp.

His whiskers had been brushed into two very definite opinions.

One ear stood perfectly upright.

The other was listening to something in the kitchen.

“Good evening.”

His voice was wonderfully proper.

I immediately regretted every time I'd ever said yeah.

“Good evening.”

“You must be our seven-thirty.”

“Yes.”

“Splendid. We've nearly finished with your table.”

He stepped aside.

Inside, a small round table was walking across the room.

Not quickly.

It had a cloth over it and was trying not to disturb a vase.

A very thin waiter followed, carrying two chairs.

The rabbit noticed me looking.

“Bit reluctant by the window. Draught, apparently.”

He closed the door gently.

“Basil,” he said. “Welcome.”

I introduced myself.

Something rustled inside his waistcoat.

He placed one hand over the pocket.

“Later.”

The rustling stopped.

I stood very still.

I didn't want to be the second thing he'd asked to wait.

Edie arrived a few minutes later.

She was wearing the green coat.

The odd button was still there.

I was pleased about that.

I gave her the flowers.

She smiled.

“Oh, these are lovely.”

They'd remembered what to say.

Basil took her coat.

The thin waiter took mine.

I hadn't been wearing one.

We had a brief discussion about the flowers instead.

His name was Geoffrey.

He said very little, but he managed to make each word sound as though it had been polished before use.

He brought a vase.

Basil glanced at the flowers.

“Did those come from the shop on the corner?”

I said yes.

He moved the vase slightly farther from a bowl of asparagus.

“Best keep them apart.”

Edie looked at me.

I looked at her.

We followed him to the table.

It had stopped walking.

Mostly.

One foot was still finding somewhere comfortable beneath the cloth.

Basil pulled out Edie's chair.

Geoffrey pulled out mine.

I sat down carefully.

The chair rose a little to meet me.

I chose not to react.

I had made the reservation.

I wanted to appear familiar with whatever I'd reserved.

The dining room was beautiful.

Dark wood.

Little lamps.

Candles that leaned away when someone passed too close.

Above us hung a chandelier with so many glass drops that looking at it made my neck feel underdressed.

There were other diners.

A woman in blue was studying a very small egg.

A man near the window was listening to his plate.

At another table, a couple were sharing something beneath a napkin.

It moved occasionally.

They seemed happy.

That reassured me.

Basil brought two menus.

They were heavy.

Mine had a little ribbon and a description of the evening that mentioned three hills and someone's grandmother.

I looked for the food.

Edie opened hers.

She turned a page.

Then turned it back.

“Have you been here before?”

“No.”

“Good.”

I looked up.

“I was hoping you hadn't understood the spoon either.”

It was silver.

Beautifully made.

Completely flat.

It would have been useful for posting something.

Then he left.

Neither of us moved.

“Everything quite all right?”

“Yes,” I said.

“Good heavens. You haven't introduced yourselves to the bread.”

“I'm Edie.”
