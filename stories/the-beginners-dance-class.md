# I Took a Beginner's Dance Class

I signed up for a beginner’s dance class because the leaflet said, “Anyone can dance.”

I have since learned that you can print absolutely anything on a leaflet.

There is no little man from the government who comes round and checks.

The class was held above a pharmacy, in a room that smelled of floor polish and people trying something new in clothes they regretted.

I wore a bow tie.

I hadn’t known what else to put on.

The woman at reception looked over the desk.

“Name?”

“Squish.”

“Any previous experience?”

“I’ve been carried quite quickly.”

She ticked a box.

I don’t know which one.

Our instructor was called Celia. She wore silver shoes and the bright, sturdy smile of someone who had already decided nothing happening tonight would be her fault.

“Welcome, everyone! There are no wrong steps here.”

A goose at the far end of the room made a small noise through his nostrils.

Celia’s smile tightened by one tooth.

“Gerald,” she said.

“Celia.”

That was the whole conversation, but there was quite a lot in it.

Gerald was tall, white, and standing so straight that I briefly wondered whether someone had posted him through the floor.

He wore a burgundy waistcoat.

A tiny gold watch hung from it on a chain.

His feet were enormous.

Not unusually enormous for a goose, probably, but once you’re about to dance with someone you do start looking at what they’ll be bringing to the floor.

He was inspecting the other students.

Not looking.

Inspecting.

I’ve been inspected before. Usually by a dog deciding whether I’m food.

This had a similar warmth.

“Let’s find partners!” Celia said.

People paired off immediately.

A married couple took each other’s hands. Two friends linked arms. A man near the door grabbed someone with the speed of a person securing the last seat on a lifeboat.

I remained where I was.

So did Gerald.

He looked at me.

Then at Celia.

Then back at me.

“Does it have feet?”

“I’m right here.”

“That wasn’t my question.”

Celia brought him over.

“Gerald, this is Squish. Squish, Gerald has danced with us before.”

“How lovely,” I said.

“Eleven years,” said Gerald.

I looked up at the banner.

BEGINNERS WELCOME.

“Long course, then.”

“I believe in mastering the fundamentals.”

Celia made a sound that began as a laugh and gave up halfway.

“Gerald will lead.”

“I assumed,” said Gerald.

He extended a wing.

I offered a little arm.

He looked at it.

“Other one.”

I offered the other one.

“No. The first one was correct.”

“Wonderful. We’re learning already.”

He took my hand.

His wing was unexpectedly soft.

I found this comforting for about three seconds.

“Don’t cling,” he said.

Celia clapped.

“We’re starting with a simple box step!”

Excellent.

I knew boxes.

I had spent a pleasant afternoon in one.

“Forward, side, together. Back, side, together.”

She demonstrated with a man called Peter, who looked delighted to have been chosen until she moved him.

Gerald watched without blinking.

“Her weight transfer is late,” he whispered.

“Shall we tell her?”

“We must live with it.”

The music began.

Gerald drew himself up.

“One, two, three.”

I moved.

“Not there.”

I moved somewhere else.

“Certainly not there.”

We stopped.

The other couples continued around us.

Gerald looked down.

“Where are your feet?”

“Still haven’t got them.”

“Yes, but where would they be?”

I thought this over.

It seemed an intimate question for the first lesson.

“Underneath, I’d imagine.”

“Then put your weight on your left one.”

I leaned left.

Too far.

My bow tie slid round to the side.

Gerald watched me sag.

“Is that deliberate?”

“Not usually.”

He lifted me upright with one wing.

“We’ll begin again.”

“One, two, three.”

I went forward.

He went forward.

We met in the middle.

It made a noise like someone sitting on a warm sponge.

Gerald closed his eyes.

“You must follow.”

“You came towards me quite confidently.”

“I’m leading.”

“Yes. Directly into me.”

He opened his eyes.

There are birds that look at you as though they’ve just noticed you.

Gerald looked at me as though he had noticed me some time ago and had now gathered enough evidence.

Celia drifted past.

“How are we getting on?”

“We are establishing basic limitations,” Gerald said.

“I’ve got several,” I added.

“Lovely! Keep it light!”

She left before either of us could explain.

Gerald adjusted his grip.

“Imagine you’re walking.”

“I don’t walk.”

“Imagine you do.”

“How well?”

“Ordinarily.”

I tried to imagine myself walking ordinarily.

I gave myself sensible shoes.

Then I wondered who had bought them.

“One,” said Gerald.

I missed it.

We started again.

This time I decided to stop thinking.

Gerald moved.

I flowed after him.

Forward.

Side.

Together.

Back.

Side.

Together.

We completed a box.

A whole one.

I looked up at him.

“Oh! We did it!”

“Don’t celebrate during the figure.”

“Sorry.”

“You’ll lose the timing.”

“Of course.”

We completed another.

I celebrated privately.

It was quite a large celebration, but I kept it inside.

Gerald’s shoulders relaxed a little.

“There,” he said. “You see?”

“Yes!”

“Something to work with.”

That pleased me more than it should have.

I am easy to encourage.

You could put “adequate” on a sticker and I’d wear it all week.

Across the room, a woman laughed as her partner turned the wrong way.

They bumped into each other, apologised, and carried on.

I smiled.

Gerald followed my gaze.

“We won’t be doing that.”

“Laughing?”

“Colliding.”

“Oh. Good.”

A pause.

“Either, if possible.”

After ten minutes, Celia asked us to change partners.

Gerald raised a wing.

“No.”

“It helps everyone develop.”

“He’s only just located his left.”

I hadn’t, actually.

I was using a small mark on the floor.

Celia looked at me.

“Are you happy staying with Gerald?”

Gerald looked down at me too.

Something in his face suggested this question had not always gone his way.

“Yes,” I said.

“He’s got a watch. It feels organised.”

Gerald straightened his waistcoat.

Celia moved on.

We stayed together.

For our next exercise, she introduced the turn.

Gerald explained that I would move beneath his raised wing.

I looked at the gap.

“That seems manageable.”

“Don’t duck.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“People duck.”

“I’d have to commit quite a lot of myself to it.”

He raised his wing.

I turned.

For one beautiful moment, everything worked.

Then I kept turning.

“Stop,” said Gerald.

I tried.

Unfortunately, I had wound myself slightly narrower at the bottom.

The top of me was still enthusiastic.

I turned once more.

And once more.

My bow tie came round.

Then the rest of me caught up.

I landed facing a fire extinguisher.

Gerald was behind me.

“Too much.”

“I did suspect.”

He turned me back.

“Single rotation.”

“I’ll tell the downstairs.”

On the next attempt, I stopped in time.

Mostly.

A small part of me continued another half turn and slapped gently against his waistcoat.

Gerald looked down.

I looked down.

“Sorry.”

He peeled me off one button.

“Core engagement,” he said.

I nodded as though I knew where mine was.

Celia announced a short break.

I wanted water.

Gerald wanted to discuss my posture.

We compromised by discussing my posture beside the water.

He produced a notebook.

A proper little notebook, with a pencil tucked down the spine.

“You take notes?”

“Of course.”

“About dancing?”

He looked at me.

I watched him write something.

“Was that about dancing?”

“Yes.”

“Was it about me?”

“Currently, those subjects overlap.”

I drank my water.

Gerald studied the page.

“You have a tendency to anticipate.”

“I’m nervous.”

“Don’t be.”

“Oh, lovely. That’s that sorted.”

He glanced up.

I wasn’t trying to be rude.

It had just come out.

His beak opened slightly.

Then shut.

He looked back at the notebook.

After a moment, he crossed something out.

I pretended not to notice.

Celia clapped again.

“Tonight, we’re going to finish with a little showcase!”

Everyone made the exact noise people make when a relaxing hobby acquires an audience.

“Nothing formal,” she said quickly. “Just a few couples at a time, showing what you’ve learned.”

Gerald’s head rose.

His entire neck seemed to gain an inch.

“How long?”

“A minute or so.”

“What music?”

“We’ve got a nice slow waltz.”

“Floor allocation?”

“Gerald.”

“I’m asking for the available space.”

“Use the whole room.”

I felt his wing tighten around mine.

Looking back, that was the moment we should have gone home.

He took me to the corner.

“We’ll need an entrance.”

“We’ve been in here forty minutes.”

“For the showcase.”

“Could we simply begin?”

He looked faintly hurt.

I wished I hadn’t said it.

“All right,” I said. “An entrance.”

He opened the notebook to a clean page.

That frightened me more than the first notebook page.

“Box step. Quarter turn. Promenade. Underarm turn. Finish.”

“I know two of those.”

“We have twelve minutes.”

He looked at the clock.

“Eleven.”

We practised.

The promenade involved moving side by side.

This was lovely.

For the first time all evening, neither of us was directly in the other’s way.

I enjoyed it so much I looked up.

Gerald did not.

He was counting.

“One, two, three. One, two, three.”

His voice had begun to sound like a grandfather clock with a personal grievance.

I tried counting too.

“One, two—”

“Not aloud.”

“Right.”

“Only one person counts.”

“Can I have Thursday?”

He made a brief noise.

I looked up sharply.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“Was that a laugh?”

“Breathing.”

“Interesting way to do it.”

He resumed counting.

But his beak had gone a little crooked.

Our turn came sooner than I would have liked.

Celia waved three couples onto the floor.

The married couple.

The two friends.

Us.

The other students sat along the wall with their water bottles.

A woman raised her phone.

Gerald saw it.

He adjusted his bow tie.

He hadn’t been wearing one before.

I don’t know where he’d kept it.

Possibly in reserve.

Celia pressed play.

The music began softly.

Gerald bowed.

I attempted a bow too.

This caused most of me to arrive at the floor before the rest.

I recovered.

There was a tiny sound from the audience.

I chose to interpret it as appreciation.

Gerald took my hand.

“One, two, three.”

We moved.

And it was good.

I mean that.

It was actually good.

We crossed the floor together, and his wing guided me, and for once I didn’t have to guess which direction he intended to be disappointed in.

Forward.

Side.

Together.

I began to feel light.

I am already fairly light, but this was a different sort.

Gerald’s feet skimmed the floor.

My bottom made a quiet squeaking noise.

It was rather peaceful.

Then he whispered, “Now.”

“Now what?”

“Promenade.”

“Oh, the sideways bit.”

“Don’t call it that.”

We turned.

We travelled.

People watched.

I saw Celia smile.

Gerald saw Celia smile.

This was unfortunate.

A sensible goose would have accepted one smile and continued.

Gerald took it as planning permission.

“Extension,” he whispered.

“We didn’t discuss an extension.”

He extended.

One wing opened magnificently.

His neck arched.

We swept towards the married couple.

The wife saw us coming and moved.

The husband did not.

She took him with her by the elbow.

His expression remained at the location where he had previously been standing.

Gerald continued.

“Turn.”

I turned.

“Again.”

“We agreed one.”

“Trust me.”

I did.

That was kind of me.

We came out of the second turn facing the wrong direction.

I knew this because the audience was now behind us and the water table was coming nearer.

“Gerald.”

“I’m aware.”

“Good.”

“Don’t stiffen.”

“I haven’t the equipment.”

He attempted a correction.

His foot landed in a small patch of water.

Nobody had spilled very much.

I want that recorded.

It was a modest amount of water.

A polite spill.

Gerald found all of it.

His left foot went sideways.

His right foot tried to retain the original appointment.

For a brief moment, he resembled a pair of scissors being opened by someone who disliked scissors.

I squeezed his wing.

“Got you!”

I hadn’t.

But it was a nice thing to say.

He leaned on me.

I spread.

One of his feet landed on top of me.

It should have stopped him.

Instead, I went with him.

We slid.

Not quickly at first.

There was time to hope.

Then his other foot joined us.

He was standing on me.

Both feet.

I was now, in a practical sense, a very worried shoe.

“Off,” I said.

“Hold your frame.”

“I’m holding most of your goose.”

We passed the water table.

Celia stood.

“Gerald?”

“Floor craft!” he called.

I don’t know why.

There was no evidence of any.

He tried to step off.

His foot stretched me.

Then slipped back.

That pushed us faster.

“Oh,” I said.

Gerald looked down.

“Oh,” he said too.

It was the first thing we’d agreed on without instruction.

We approached the mirrors.

I had never seen myself coming towards myself at speed.

I looked quite concerned.

Gerald put out a wing and caught the ballet barre.

For a wonderful second, we stopped.

Then my stretched side pulled taut.

Gerald looked at me.

I looked at Gerald.

“No,” he said.

I let go.

We shot backwards.

His wing dragged along the barre with a rapid little ticking sound.

We crossed the room in a straight line.

It was our cleanest travelling step of the evening.

The woman with the phone stood on her chair.

Celia reached the music player.

“I’m stopping the music!”

“Don’t!” Gerald shouted.

I could have hit him.

I couldn’t reach that far up.

He caught my arms.

I caught his waistcoat.

We turned.

I’m not certain either of us chose to.

A chair had become involved at the edge.

We swung round it and emerged facing the room again.

Gerald was now leaning forward.

I was leaning backwards.

We had accidentally made a pose.

The audience clapped.

Gerald heard applause.

I felt him make a decision.

“No,” I whispered.

“Follow.”

“No, I know that tone now.”

He lifted one foot.

I wobbled.

He put it back.

The audience clapped harder.

They thought it was a comic routine.

I would like to stress that I was doing my best.

Gerald swept a wing towards them.

This took away half our steering.

We headed for the refreshment table.

There was a plate of biscuits on it.

Gerald saw the biscuits.

His head moved before the rest of him.

He snatched one as we passed.

I stared up at him.

“Really?”

He tried to answer.

His beak was full.

“You had time for a biscuit?”

He swallowed.

“Low blood sugar.”

We hit the corner of the table.

Not hard.

Just enough to send the plate into the air.

Gerald caught it.

This was a mistake.

It would have been much easier to forgive him if he’d dropped it.

But he caught it neatly, one wing beneath the plate, the other still holding me.

The room erupted.

Someone cheered.

Gerald’s eyes changed.

I have seen a dog find an open bin.

It was that.

“Oh, please don’t enjoy this,” I said.

He bowed to the audience.

While moving.

With biscuits.

We clipped the edge of a coat rack.

A long pink scarf floated down across his shoulders.

He tossed his head to clear it.

The scarf settled beautifully.

I hated how well it suited him.

Celia stood in the centre of the room with both hands up.

“Slow down!”

“Trying!” I called.

Gerald called, “Final figure!”

We had not agreed a final figure.

We had agreed a finish.

A finish is where something stops.

I was beginning to understand Gerald had never quite accepted the distinction.

He lowered the biscuit plate onto an empty chair as we passed.

Then he drew me up.

I stretched between his wings.

“Prepare.”

“For what?”

“A dip.”

“Gerald, I am nearly all dip already.”

He leaned me backwards.

I went.

And went.

My back touched the floor.

Then my head.

Then, because he was still moving, the rest of me followed underneath him.

Gerald stepped forward.

Onto me.

Again.

“Oh, not the shoes,” I said.

We slid towards Celia.

Her smile was gone.

I didn’t blame her.

She had lent it to thirty beginners and Gerald had driven over it.

“Gerald,” she said, very calmly, “put both wings out.”

He did.

I did too.

I don’t know why.

I wanted to be useful.

Celia caught one wing.

Peter, the demonstration man, caught the other.

Gerald stopped.

I continued.

Only a little.

Enough to stretch.

Enough to know what would happen next.

“Oh, dear,” I said.

Then I sprang back.

I travelled up Gerald’s waistcoat.

Past the watch.

Past the bow tie.

And landed on his head.

The music ended.

Exactly then.

One last grand chord.

Gerald stood between Celia and Peter with his wings spread wide and me draped over his skull like an unusually anxious beret.

The pink scarf settled.

A biscuit rolled slowly across the floor.

Nobody spoke.

From the top of Gerald’s head, I said, “Do we bow now?”

The room lost its mind.

People were laughing so hard the woman on the chair had to sit down on it.

The married man was folded over his knees.

His wife was hitting his back, although I couldn’t tell whether she was helping or just expressing herself.

Peter turned away.

His shoulders shook.

Even Celia covered her mouth.

Gerald stood completely still.

I slid down to his shoulder.

“Are you hurt?”

“No.”

“Angry?”

He looked at the audience.

Then at the scarf.

Then at me.

A small sound came out of his beak.

I waited.

Another sound followed.

It was a horrible noise.

Like a bicycle horn discovering bad news.

Gerald was laughing.

Properly laughing.

His neck bent.

His eyes shut.

He made three short honks and had to put a wing against the wall.

I started laughing too.

I couldn’t help it.

He tried to speak.

Failed.

Looked at the biscuit on the floor.

Started again.

We stood there for quite a while.

Eventually, Celia handed us our water.

“Well,” she said.

Gerald dabbed at one eye with the scarf.

“It travelled.”

“Yes,” said Celia. “It certainly did.”

She looked at me.

“First class?”

I nodded.

“Any questions?”

“Does the pharmacy downstairs sell something for this?”

“For what?”

I gestured vaguely at everything.

At the end of the evening, Gerald found his notebook.

He opened it.

I braced myself.

There would be pages.

There might be diagrams.

He looked at what he’d written earlier.

Then he tore the page out.

Folded it once.

And put it in his pocket.

“What are you writing now?” I asked.

He angled the notebook away.

“Nothing.”

I peeked.

He’d written my name.

Then, underneath:

NEXT THURSDAY.

“You want to dance with me again?”

“If you’re available.”

“I am.”

“Good.”

He tucked the pencil away.

“We’ll simplify the middle section.”

I stared at him.

“The bit where I became footwear?”

“It lacked control.”

“It lacked a consent form.”

His beak went crooked again.

Celia approached with something pinched between two fingers.

“Gerald. Before you go.”

It was the pink scarf.

He looked down at his shoulders.

“Oh.”

“It belongs to Margaret.”

Margaret stood by the door.

She studied him for a moment.

Then she waved a hand.

“Keep it.”

Gerald touched the scarf.

“Are you sure?”

“Looked better on you.”

He went very quiet.

Then he thanked her in a voice I hadn’t heard all evening.

We walked downstairs together.

Well.

He walked.

I came along.

Outside the pharmacy, he stopped and checked his watch.

“I’m usually home by now.”

“Sorry.”

He shook his head.

“Fish and chips?”

I looked up.

“With me?”

“No, I’m asking the postbox.”

“Still a bit sharp for a beginner, Gerald.”

He held the door open with one wing.

I went through.

Next Thursday, I brought him a biscuit.

Just one.

In a little bag.

He looked pleased.

Then he opened his waistcoat and showed me what he’d brought.

Knee pads.

“You haven’t got knees,” I said.

“They’re for you.”

“I haven’t either.”

“Yes,” said Gerald. “But we’ll find somewhere.”

From across the room, Celia saw us.

She looked at the bag.

Then at the knee pads.

Then at Gerald’s pink scarf.

Very slowly, she moved the water table into the cupboard.
