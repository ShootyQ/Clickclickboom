# The Aquarium Complaints Desk

I once worked the complaints desk at an aquarium.

For the fish.

That bit wasn’t explained until I’d already put my lunch in the fridge.

The fridge was in the staffroom.

The staffroom was underwater.

There were several things I should have asked.

My friend Terry got me the job.

Terry fixes things.

Mostly things he’s been asked not to touch, but he’s quick and he has a van.

He said the aquarium needed someone who was good with people.

I said, “Am I?”

He said, “You don’t interrupt.”

That’s because I’m usually trying to work out whether I’ve been included in the conversation.

But apparently it counted.

He told me to arrive before opening, wear something smart, and ask for Elaine.

Elaine was the manager.

Practical shoes.

Keys on a long blue string.

A woman who could tell whether a penguin was up to something without looking directly at it.

She gave me a polo shirt.

Blue.

With a little embroidered seahorse.

I looked quite good.

Until the shirt slid down and I became a blue bag with a face.

Elaine tucked the sleeves in.

Stepped back.

Said, “That’ll do.”

A phrase that has put me in considerable danger over the years.

Then she took me downstairs.

Past the jellyfish.

Past a room full of pumps.

Past a man holding a bucket very carefully.

I asked what was in it.

He said, “Don’t make me look again.”

I left him to it.

At the end of the corridor was a large tank.

Inside the tank was a desk.

Two chairs.

A telephone.

And a little sign.

WE’RE LISTENING.

Elaine pointed.

“That’s you.”

I looked at the tank.

Then at her.

Then back at the tank.

I said, “Is there a dry version?”

She said no.

I said, “Do I get a diving suit?”

She looked at me.

“Do you need one?”

I thought about that.

I’d never really checked.

That’s the trouble with having no obvious organs.

Everyone assumes you’re available for things.

They lowered me in.

The desk was bolted down.

The chair wasn’t.

I sat.

It rose slowly past my face.

I watched it go.

Elaine watched from above.

Then she shouted:

“We’ve been meaning to fix that!”

An eel came out from behind the desk.

He looked at the chair.

Looked at me.

Then said:

“Still?”

His name was Vince.

He’d made the first complaint.

About the chair.

Seven months earlier.

I asked why an eel needed a chair.

He said, “I don’t. But that’s not the point.”

I knew then he was going to be a regular.

Elaine explained my duties through a microphone.

Listen to the residents.

Make a note of their concerns.

Resolve what I could.

Bring anything serious to her.

I asked what counted as serious.

She thought for a moment.

Then said:

“If the water’s on the wrong side of something.”

Useful.

Clear.

I liked her.

Before leaving, she pointed at a red handle beside the desk.

“Don’t pull that.”

I asked what it did.

She said, “Makes the day longer.”

Then she went upstairs.

At nine, the aquarium opened.

I knew because a small child pressed an entire sausage roll against the glass.

Not offering it.

Just showing it.

An enormous display of confidence from someone with ketchup on his ear.

The child saw me.

Stopped chewing.

Then called his mother over.

She looked at me.

Looked at the sign.

Said:

“I think that one cleans the tank.”

I had been employed for eleven minutes.

Already promoted.

My first proper customer was a stingray.

He arrived just above the floor.

Moved his mouth a little.

Then said, “I want the name changed.”

I picked up my pencil.

“What’s your name?”

“Colin.”

I said, “What would you prefer?”

He looked at me.

“Not Colin. Stingray.”

I put the pencil down.

Colin said he’d been called a stingray his entire life and had never stung anyone.

Not once.

He’d been stepped on twice.

A child had tried to put a sticker on him.

Someone had once called him a “wet pancake” directly to his face.

Still nothing.

“I’ve shown restraint,” he said.

I agreed.

He wanted something friendlier.

He’d brought suggestions.

Glider.

Sea kite.

Colin.

I said the last one might be difficult to apply to the whole species.

He said:

“Fine. The others can choose their own.”

I told him I’d speak to Elaine.

He seemed pleased.

Then, before leaving, he asked whether the touch pool was compulsory.

I said I didn’t know.

He said:

“Because I’ve touched nobody.”

Then he slid away.

I wrote that down.

It felt worth keeping.

Next came an octopus.

He arrived carrying a mug, a piece of pipe, and what appeared to be the key to the gift shop.

I asked if that was the key to the gift shop.

He looked at it.

Then at me.

Then moved it to another arm.

“No.”

His name was Bernard.

He wanted eight lunch breaks.

I said, “Eight?”

He nodded.

“One per arm.”

I said I wasn’t sure that was how lunch worked.

Bernard laid one arm flat on the desk.

Then another.

Then another.

By the sixth, there wasn’t much desk left.

“Look at the workload,” he said.

I looked.

It was persuasive.

I asked whether all the arms ate separately.

He said, “Do all your employees think separately?”

I said I’d only just started.

He said:

“You’ll see.”

Then he asked if I could sign something.

I asked what it was.

He said it was just to confirm we’d spoken.

The paper was inside a plastic sleeve.

I read the top.

AUTHORISATION FOR EIGHT LUNCH BREAKS.

I looked up.

Bernard looked at me.

Then he said:

“That’s the wrong side.”

He turned it over.

The other side said:

WE HAVE DEFINITELY SPOKEN.

I didn’t sign either.

He took it well.

For an octopus, that means he left with only one of my pencils.

At ten, the shark arrived.

Nigel.

Big fellow.

Grey suit, naturally.

A lot of teeth.

He stopped on the other side of the glass partition.

There were limits to how open we wanted the office.

He didn’t look angry.

He looked as though he’d been practicing what to say on the way over.

I asked if he wanted to sit.

Then remembered the chair.

It was resting against the ceiling.

Nigel looked up at it.

Said, “I’m all right, thanks.”

I asked what was wrong.

He said:

“The announcement.”

I waited.

He looked toward the loudspeaker.

Then, in a horrible, cheerful voice, he said:

“IT’S FEEDING TIME FOR NIGEL!”

I recognized it.

I’d heard it coming in.

Very enthusiastic.

A voice normally used to tell children a bouncy castle is ready.

Nigel said they did it twice a day.

Every day.

“People come running.”

I said that was probably good.

He looked at me.

“To watch me have lunch.”

I considered that.

He said:

“They don’t announce when Elaine has a sandwich.”

Fair.

He said it made him sound as though eating was the only thing he did.

Like he spent the rest of the day waiting beside the spoon drawer.

“I have interests,” he said.

I asked what they were.

He hesitated.

Then said:

“Other things.”

I nodded.

Didn’t rush him.

He said he’d once watched a whole documentary about railway stations.

Through the staffroom window.

He hadn’t understood all of it.

But he’d enjoyed the roofs.

I wrote that down.

Not as a complaint.

Just so I’d remember.

Then the loudspeaker crackled.

A woman’s voice said:

“Good morning, visitors! Don’t forget, at eleven o’clock—”

Nigel closed his eyes.

“—it’s FEEDING TIME FOR NIGEL!”

A child screamed.

Not in fear.

In anticipation.

Nigel opened his eyes.

Looked at me.

Then said:

“I’m forty-three.”

I promised I’d talk to Elaine.

He thanked me.

Very quietly.

Then he swam away, which is how sharks leave you with a lot to think about.

By eleven, there was a queue.

A seahorse wanted the photographer to stop asking whether he was the mother.

A crab had lost a claw and wanted to know whether lost property was being thorough.

A flatfish had been mistaken for the floor so often that someone had put a chair on him.

I said I was sorry.

He said:

“You’re not the first.”

Then Vince came back.

The eel.

He asked whether I’d made progress on the chair.

I pointed up.

He looked.

Said:

“So, no.”

By lunchtime, I had filled four pages.

My pencil was soft.

The pages were soft.

I was soft.

The whole office had become a difficult place to establish which things were meant to be.

I went upstairs to find Elaine.

This involved a little lifting platform.

You got on.

Pressed a button.

Rose out of the water.

Very nice.

Bernard was already on it.

He was wearing a visitor sticker.

I asked where he was going.

He said:

“Lunch.”

I asked which one.

He didn’t answer.

A lot of his strength lay in not answering.

Elaine was eating beside the penguin enclosure.

A penguin was watching her sandwich.

She moved it left.

The penguin moved left.

She moved it right.

The penguin moved right.

She looked at me.

Said:

“Don’t ever teach them your name.”

Then she stood up, put the sandwich under her arm, and listened.

I told her about Colin.

She nodded.

About Nigel.

She frowned.

About Bernard.

She looked past me.

“Where is Bernard?”

I turned.

The lifting platform was empty.

The visitor sticker was stuck to a wall.

Elaine put her sandwich down.

The penguin took it.

Neither of us had time to object.

We found Bernard in the café.

Under a table.

Holding eight chips.

I looked at him.

He looked at me.

“One per arm,” he said.

Elaine crouched down.

“Bernard.”

He looked at the chips.

Then at her.

“Six now, technically.”

We took him back.

He complained that his break had been interrupted.

Elaine said he hadn’t been on a break.

He said that was exactly the problem.

On the way, he tried to take a muffin.

Not secretly.

He reached for it while we were watching.

I said, “We can see you.”

He looked genuinely disappointed in the arm.

Then withdrew it.

After lunch, Elaine showed me the announcement system.

It was in a cupboard.

Most important things in public buildings are in cupboards.

There were buttons for each exhibit.

Penguins.

Reef.

Jellyfish.

Nigel.

Nigel had his own button.

It was yellow.

Someone had drawn teeth around it.

I didn’t tell him that.

There were prerecorded announcements, and a microphone for live ones.

Elaine said I could make a short announcement that afternoon.

Explain a few things.

Give the visitors a better understanding.

I said:

“About the fish?”

She said:

“Ideally.”

Then she handed me the microphone.

It had a foam cover.

I touched it.

It felt like a cousin.

I put it down.

That afternoon, I tried helping Nigel find something else he liked.

Not because he needed a hobby to deserve a quiet lunch.

Everyone deserves that.

But he’d mentioned the railway stations with such care.

As if he wasn’t sure I’d let him keep them.

So I asked him to show me around his tank.

He brightened up.

Immediately.

There was a lot he knew.

Where the light hit first in the morning.

Which rock had been moved last winter.

Where the little fish gathered when they wanted him to swim past and stir the water.

He showed me a corner where a pipe made a tiny clicking sound.

Said it happened every seventeen seconds.

He’d counted.

He showed me a bit of glass with a scratch.

“Third panel,” he said. “Near the bottom.”

I asked how he’d noticed it.

He said:

“I live here.”

Of course.

Then a group of children arrived.

Nigel turned toward them.

Smiled.

Not a good choice, with his equipment.

Three children stepped back.

One hid behind a teacher.

Another said:

“Is he going to eat us?”

Nigel stopped smiling.

Slowly closed his mouth.

Looked away.

I knew that look.

You can be bright red and have a friendly face and still get mistaken for a spill.

I climbed up onto a little ledge where the children could see me.

The teacher leaned closer.

Said:

“Oh, look. A cleaner.”

Twice in one day.

At that point, I thought I should ask about the pay difference.

I waved.

The children waved back.

I pointed at Nigel.

Then at the scratched bit of glass.

Then I gave Nigel a thumbs-up.

Not literally.

I did my best with the available edges.

Nigel looked confused.

But he came over.

Stopped beside the scratch.

The children leaned in.

One little girl pointed.

She’d seen it.

Nigel nodded.

The girl looked pleased.

Then she pointed at another mark.

Nigel looked.

Moved closer.

Inspected it.

Shook his head.

Not a scratch.

Just something on the outside.

The girl rubbed it with her sleeve.

It came off.

Nigel gave a slow, approving nod.

For the next five minutes, the children inspected the glass with him.

No screaming.

No “feeding frenzy.”

Just a large shark and twelve small people checking a window.

Not at me.

At Nigel.

“That was quite good.”

Head up.

“Worth asking.”

I didn’t ask.

“Good afternoon, everyone.”

Very warm.

Very still.

Then he gave me a little flap.

Next, I mentioned Nigel.

Like theirs.

He also spent his day watching the tank, checking his surroundings, and taking an interest in things.

Looking.
