# I Went to Buy a Light Bulb

I went to buy a light bulb because the kitchen had gone dark.

It had happened halfway through making toast.

One moment, I could see the toast.

The next, I had to believe in it.

I finished breakfast by the light of the open fridge, which was pleasant until the milk started looking concerned.

Then I decided to sort it out.

Immediately.

That is the sort of person I intended to become that morning.

I took out the dead bulb and wrapped it in a tea towel.

I photographed the fitting.

I photographed the kitchen.

Then I wrote KITCHEN on a piece of paper and put that in the bag as well.

I didn’t know whether they would need it.

But I wanted them to understand I had come with a location in mind.

The shop was called BRIGHT IDEAS.

There was a light bulb over the door.

Even their sign had one.

I found that reassuring.

Inside, there were hundreds.

Bulbs hanging from the ceiling.

Bulbs standing on tables.

Bulbs illuminating other bulbs, presumably so you could see how good the first bulbs were doing.

I had never been anywhere so thoroughly able to see itself.

A man approached.

He was tall and narrow, with glasses on a cord and a tape measure clipped to his belt.

His badge said COLIN.

Underneath, it said:

ASK ME ABOUT LIGHT.

I intended to keep him within his comfort zone.

“Hello,” I said. “I need a light bulb.”

Colin smiled.

“What sort?”

I took the tea towel out of my bag.

He watched me unwrap it.

I had folded the towel rather carefully, and for a moment it looked as though I might be bringing him a tiny injury.

“This sort.”

Colin took it.

Turned it over.

Held it against a lamp.

Then he made a noise.

Not a large noise.

Just enough to suggest I should have brought a relative.

“They don’t make these anymore.”

“It was working on Tuesday.”

“Yes,” said Colin. “A lot has changed.”

I looked at the bulb.

Tuesday had been quite ordinary.

I wished I’d known.

We might have done something.

Colin turned it again.

“Where was this being used?”

“The kitchen.”

I gave him the piece of paper.

He looked at it.

Then at me.

“I’ve written it down.”

“Yes. I can see.”

“In case it helps.”

He placed the paper beside the bulb.

I appreciated him taking it seriously.

“What temperature are you after?”

I considered the question.

“Comfortable.”

Colin waited.

“I do make toast, but I don’t need it heated from above.”

“Colour temperature.”

“Oh.”

“Warm or cool?”

I thought of the fridge.

“Warm, I suppose. I’ve tried the other.”

“Do you know your existing specification?”

I pointed at the bulb.

“Deceased.”

Colin took off his glasses.

Put them back on.

“I’ll show you some options.”

That sounded helpful.

It was the last thing that did for quite a while.

He led me to a display divided into three little rooms.

None of the rooms was large enough to enter.

One contained a miniature chair.

One contained a miniature kitchen.

The last contained a tiny man standing beside a tiny desk, already looking tired of his job.

Colin pressed a switch.

“This is warm white.”

The little kitchen glowed gently.

“Lovely.”

He pressed another.

“Cool white.”

The kitchen became somewhere you might be asked to identify a handbag.

“Less lovely.”

“And daylight.”

The third room lit up.

I looked down at myself.

I had become a medical discovery.

Colin studied me.

“Excellent colour rendering.”

“I can see several things I hadn’t asked to know.”

He switched back to warm white.

My usual red returned.

“That one,” I said. “The one where I look as though I’m going to recover.”

Colin nodded.

“Warm white.”

“Wonderful. I’ll have—”

“Now, brightness.”

Of course.

I had been foolish to think we’d reached light bulb yet.

Colin asked how many lumens I wanted.

I told him I’d only come for one.

He explained.

Patiently.

I tried to look like a person receiving useful information rather than someone watching a simple errand develop stairs.

“How big is the kitchen?” he asked.

I showed him the photograph.

He enlarged it.

Studied the ceiling.

Then the counter.

Then the floor.

“Is that your only light source?”

“There’s the fridge.”

He looked up.

I wished I hadn’t mentioned it.

“And this window?”

“Also useful, but unreliable after sunset.”

A woman beside us leaned over.

“North-facing?”

“I’ve never asked it.”

She nodded gravely.

“That’ll be your problem.”

I looked at Colin.

He hadn’t asked this woman to join us.

Neither had I.

Nevertheless, she had arrived with a verdict.

She wore a green coat and held a packet of bulbs against her chest like she was protecting them from disappointment.

“Mine faces south,” she said.

“Congratulations.”

“Too much glare.”

“I’m sorry.”

She examined my old bulb.

“Oh, you won’t get another one of those.”

“Colin mentioned.”

“I had a lovely bulb in 1994.”

She paused.

We waited.

There appeared to be more.

“Proper light.”

Colin nodded with the careful gentleness of a man who had heard about this bulb before.

“Mrs Pritchard.”

“Don’t tell me. They’ve all improved.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“Well, you were thinking it.”

Colin turned back to my photograph.

“What do you mainly do in the kitchen?”

“Toast.”

“Preparation or consumption?”

I looked at him.

He looked back.

Both seemed to matter to him.

“I hadn’t realised I needed separate lighting for those.”

“You’d be surprised.”

I was becoming very easy to surprise.

Colin indicated the counter.

“Task lighting would help here.”

I indicated the whole photograph.

“I was hoping this bulb could take on several tasks.”

“What sort of atmosphere do you want?”

“Kitchen.”

“Yes, but how would you like it to feel?”

“Occupied by someone who can see.”

He nodded.

Mrs Pritchard nodded too.

I had accidentally said something they could work with.

Colin moved us towards another display.

This one contained a wooden bowl, a small plant, and an arrangement of pears that looked as though they had been placed by someone who did not trust pears to organise themselves.

He switched on a lamp.

The light was soft.

The pears looked expensive.

“Oh,” I said. “That’s nice.”

Colin stopped.

He turned to me.

“Nice?”

“Just generally.”

“What do you like about it?”

I hadn’t expected to defend nice.

“The visibility, mainly.”

He pressed a button on a little radio clipped to his shirt.

“Marion? Could you join us in lifestyle?”

I looked round.

We were, apparently, in lifestyle.

I had thought we were beside a bowl.

A woman appeared carrying a fabric sample book.

I didn’t know why she had fabric.

I didn’t know why I had been there long enough for a second member of staff.

“Colin says you’re interested in creating a warmer kitchen.”

“He’s taken that quite far.”

“I’m Marion.”

“Squish.”

“Lovely. Shall we sit?”

She brought over a chair.

Nobody had offered me a chair when this was still a purchase.

I sat because she had brought it and now I felt responsible for its journey.

Marion sat opposite.

Colin stood beside her holding my dead bulb.

It looked like I was being told why we hadn’t been able to save it.

“Tell me,” she said, “how do you want to feel when you enter your kitchen?”

I considered giving a good answer.

Something mature.

Perhaps relaxed.

Perhaps welcome.

But I was tired.

“I’d mostly like to see whether anyone’s already in it.”

Marion nodded.

“Security.”

“No, not—”

She wrote it down.

Colin nodded too.

Mrs Pritchard had sat on a display stool.

She was staying.

“Do you entertain?” Marion asked.

“I’m told I do.”

“At home.”

“Occasionally, but nobody’s ever mentioned the bulb.”

“They wouldn’t,” said Mrs Pritchard.

I turned.

“Wouldn’t they?”

“Oh, no.”

I thought about every person who had ever eaten at my kitchen table.

All that silence.

All those secret opinions about my overhead lighting.

“Would you call your kitchen a social space?” Marion asked.

“I’ve apologised to a kettle in there.”

She smiled.

I smiled back.

We were getting nowhere, but pleasantly.

Then Colin said, “Perhaps we should demonstrate the smart range.”

Marion’s smile widened.

Mrs Pritchard made a noise into her packet.

I should have sided with her.

Colin picked up his phone.

“This lets you control the lighting remotely.”

“How remotely?”

“From anywhere.”

“I’m usually in the kitchen when I need the kitchen light.”

“You can set routines.”

“I’ve already got one. I go in and turn it on.”

He gave a small laugh.

Not because I’d made a joke.

Because he thought I was being modest about my ambitions.

“Let me show you.”

He tapped the screen.

A lamp across the shop turned purple.

A second lamp dimmed.

A bathroom mirror said, “Good afternoon.”

I looked at the mirror.

It was not a good time for objects to begin speaking.

“Could you bring that back to warm white?” Marion asked.

Colin tapped again.

The purple lamp went blue.

The mirror said, “Light rain expected.”

“Not yours, Colin,” Marion said. “The customer profile.”

“I’m on the customer profile.”

“No, that’s seasonal.”

“Why would seasonal be in kitchen?”

Mrs Pritchard leaned towards me.

“My bulb didn’t need a profile.”

“For once,” I said, “I’m fully with you.”

A voice came from a small speaker.

“New device detected. Would you like to pair?”

“No, thank you,” I said.

There was a pause.

“Pairing.”

I looked at Colin.

“It’s misunderstood me.”

“It’s voice-activated.”

“It’s not voice-obedient.”

The lamp beside me flashed green.

Then pink.

Then a rather personal shade of red.

Marion checked her tablet.

“Oh. You’re on romance.”

“I’d like to be on the bus.”

Colin tapped hurriedly.

“Normal lighting.”

The mirror said, “I don’t understand.”

Neither did I, but I wasn’t mounted on the wall announcing it.

A man at the till called over.

“Colin, have you got the pendant?”

“Not now, Trevor.”

“Well, someone’s turned my island off.”

I looked up.

There were no islands.

I was willing to believe anything by then.

Colin pressed something.

Half the display went dark.

The other half became blue.

The mirror announced that sunset would be at six seventeen.

Mrs Pritchard looked at her watch.

“At this rate, we’ll see it.”

I slid off the chair.

Very quietly.

Marion was busy explaining a lighting scene to Colin.

Colin was busy arguing with a mirror.

It seemed an excellent opportunity to select a bulb myself.

I found a plain cardboard box on a low shelf.

No app.

No photograph of people enjoying their improved lives.

Just a drawing of a bulb.

The price was reasonable.

The shape was familiar.

It even looked a little like mine.

I picked it up.

I cannot adequately describe the relief.

I had found something in the shop that had not asked me a question.

I took it to the till.

Trevor looked up.

“Find everything you needed?”

“No, but I’ve decided this is it.”

He took the box.

Turned it over.

Paused.

I was becoming familiar with the pause.

“Where did you get this?”

“Over there.”

He looked over there.

Then back at the box.

“This is for the sign.”

“What sign?”

“The shop sign.”

I looked towards the door.

BRIGHT IDEAS glowed above the window.

Trevor pointed.

“Replacement stock. Shouldn’t have been on the shelf.”

I held out a little hand.

“Can I buy it anyway?”

He hesitated.

“It’s quite specific.”

“So am I, at the moment.”

“It gives a very narrow beam.”

“My toast isn’t enormous.”

Colin arrived beside us.

“Oh, no. That wouldn’t be suitable.”

Mrs Pritchard appeared at his elbow.

“It’s the best bulb in the building.”

Trevor looked at her.

She looked back.

“I know light.”

I pictured the shop sign hanging in my kitchen.

It would take up most of the wall.

But I would be able to see.

Visitors would know where I bought it.

And Colin might stop asking about my lifestyle.

“How much of the sign would I need?” I asked.

Colin put a hand on the counter.

“Let’s take a breath.”

“I’ve been taking them all morning.”

He picked up my old bulb again.

“Now. The straightforward replacement would be this.”

He pointed to a small shelf behind the till.

There was a price label.

Above it, a perfectly empty hook.

We all looked at it.

Then Colin bent down and checked underneath.

I’m not sure what he expected.

A shy bulb, perhaps.

Trevor tapped the computer.

“None in stock.”

“Delivery?” Colin asked.

“Thursday.”

I looked at the empty hook.

Then at Colin.

“How long has it been out of stock?”

Trevor looked at the screen.

“Since yesterday.”

Nobody said anything.

From the back of the shop, the mirror announced that traffic was heavier than usual.

Colin cleared his throat.

“We’ve been exploring options.”

“I’d noticed.”

“And we have established your preferences.”

“Yes,” I said. “I’ve become particularly attached to buying a bulb.”

Marion joined us.

“Any luck?”

“Not in stock,” Trevor said.

“Oh, that’s a shame.”

I waited.

Everyone seemed to feel it was a shame.

No one seemed to feel it had been a shame for quite some time.

Mrs Pritchard touched my arm.

“There’s another shop near the station.”

I looked at her.

“Does it have bulbs?”

“Some.”

That was now an exciting range.

I turned towards the door.

And saw the floor lamp.

It stood beside a sofa in the window.

Tall.

Slightly crooked.

An enormous cream shade.

It was on.

I could see it being on.

No demonstration was required.

No one needed to describe how it might feel once switched on.

It had already committed.

“How much is that?” I asked.

Colin followed my gaze.

“The floor lamp?”

“Yes.”

“That’s a display model.”

“I’m looking at it.”

“It’s reduced.”

“How reduced?”

He told me.

It cost more than a bulb.

Considerably more.

But less than spending another morning with Colin.

“I’ll take it.”

Marion brightened.

“It’s a lovely piece.”

“Does it stay on?”

“Sorry?”

“If I take it home and plug it in, will it continue doing that?”

“Yes.”

“Then we’ve found my lifestyle.”

Trevor rang it through.

Colin reached for the plug.

“Not yet,” I said.

He stopped.

“I’ll just disconnect—”

“Receipt first.”

We waited for the printer.

I wanted evidence that the working light and I belonged to each other before anyone interfered.

Trevor handed me the receipt.

Only then did I let Colin unplug it.

The lamp went dark.

I felt a brief but powerful sense of betrayal.

“It’ll come back,” Colin said.

“It had better.”

They wrapped the shade in paper.

The shade was much wider than me.

Carrying it made me look like a little red foot beneath an umbrella.

Colin brought the base.

Marion brought a leaflet about layering light.

I left that on the counter.

I was already at my maximum number of layers.

Mrs Pritchard held the door.

“Good luck,” she said.

“You too.”

She looked down at her packet of bulbs.

“Oh, I’m not buying these.”

I stopped.

“What?”

“Returning them.”

We considered each other.

Then she looked back into the shop.

“They’re too cheerful.”

I left before she could involve me.

The bus driver saw the lamp first.

Then the little bit of me visible underneath.

He lowered his head.

“Where’s that going?”

“Home, hopefully.”

“Can’t block the aisle.”

I got it onto the bus.

Then I got myself onto the bus.

These were separate stages.

We sat in the space near the front.

The lamp took the window.

I didn’t argue.

It had cost enough to enjoy the view.

A small child opposite watched us for three stops.

Then he asked his mother why the lamp had a face.

She told him not to stare.

I appreciated that.

I was having a difficult day, but I still wanted to be treated as furniture with dignity.

By the time I got home, I had carried the lamp through two doors, apologised to a woman with a shopping trolley, and briefly become tangled with a man delivering a mattress.

The mattress man said, “You should’ve got a smaller one.”

I said, “It was originally meant to fit in a tea towel.”

He chose not to pursue it.

The kitchen had not improved in my absence.

I stood the lamp beside the counter.

Its shade filled most of the doorway.

Its base occupied the space where I usually stood to make toast.

I moved it.

Now I could reach the toaster, but not the drawer.

I moved it again.

Now the drawer opened.

Into the lamp.

I stood still for a while.

The lamp stood still too.

It was excellent at its part.

Eventually, I placed it beside the kitchen table.

Then I ran the cable round a chair leg and plugged it in.

The light came on.

Warm.

Steady.

Beautiful.

I sat beneath it.

Half the kitchen disappeared behind the shade.

The other half looked like a room where somebody was about to confess something.

I made toast.

Sat down.

Looked at my reflection in the dark window.

I looked magnificent.

The light caught one side of my face and left the other gently mysterious.

I had cheekbones.

Not actual cheekbones.

But a strong suggestion that I knew someone who did.

I turned a little.

Even better.

This was irritating.

Colin had been right about one thing.

I ate my toast slowly.

In case anyone was watching.

Then I noticed the label tied to the lamp’s stem.

BULB INCLUDED.

Underneath was a little diagram.

I stared at it.

The fitting looked familiar.

Very familiar.

I fetched the tea towel.

Unwrapped the old bulb.

Held it beside the diagram.

Same shape.

Same base.

Same two little pins.

I looked up into the lampshade.

Then at the empty kitchen fitting.

Then back into the lampshade.

I unplugged the lamp.

Waited for the bulb to cool.

I was not giving Colin the satisfaction of a burn.

Then I took it out.

Carried it over.

Put it into the ceiling fitting.

It clicked.

I stepped down.

Pressed the switch.

The kitchen lit up.

All of it.

The counter.

The drawer.

The table.

My second slice of toast, which had been on the floor for some time.

I stood in the doorway.

Empty floor lamp beside me.

Entire morning behind me.

I could have been angry.

But I was finally in the right lighting for it.

That evening, Colin called.

Just a courtesy follow-up, apparently.

“How are you finding the lamp?”

I looked at it.

Without its bulb, it was a tall and rather expensive obstacle.

“I know exactly where it is.”

“And are you happy with the effect?”

I looked up at the kitchen ceiling.

“Yes.”

“Excellent.”

He sounded genuinely pleased.

I let him have that.

Then he said, “Of course, if you’d like something complementary for the other side of the room—”

I hung up.

Gently.

I didn’t want to disturb the atmosphere.
