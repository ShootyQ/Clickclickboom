# I Wanted a Sandwich Named After Me

I once tried to get a sandwich named after me.

Not a statue.

Not a road.

I had thought carefully about what I could reasonably achieve.

A sandwich seemed within reach.

It started at Rita’s café, where I was having a perfectly ordinary lunch until a man at the next table said, “Big Dennis, please.”

Rita wrote it down.

“Brown or white?”

“White.”

That interested me.

I hadn’t known you could order a Dennis.

I looked at the menu.

There he was.

BIG DENNIS.

Ham, mature cheddar, mustard.

Below him was LITTLE JEAN.

Chicken, bacon, mayonnaise.

I looked round the café with a new respect.

Some of these people might be lunch.

“Is there a Dennis?” I asked Rita.

She pointed towards the window.

A man in a checked shirt raised his hand.

He was eating soup.

“Why aren’t you having yours?” I asked.

“Had one yesterday.”

He went back to his soup.

Imagine being that secure.

I asked about Jean.

Rita pointed to a woman beside the radiator.

Jean was considerably larger than Dennis.

I decided to learn the system before commenting.

“How do you get a sandwich named after you?”

Rita wiped the counter.

“Come in for years. Order something we end up putting on the menu.”

“How many years?”

“It isn’t a points scheme.”

“Of course.”

I nodded.

I had hoped it was a points scheme.

A points scheme gives you somewhere to stand.

“Anyone managed it quickly?”

“Keith suggested the pickle.”

At a table near the back, a quiet man looked up.

“Did you want the jar?”

“No, Keith. Just saying.”

He nodded and returned to his lunch.

I studied him.

Grey jumper.

Glasses.

One of those faces that looked as though it had always remembered to bring a bag.

He didn’t seem particularly ambitious.

That was what made him dangerous.

I went home thinking about the menu.

There was something wonderful about the idea.

Someone hungry would come in, look at all their options, and choose me.

Not actual me.

I want that clear.

I had no plans to enter the food chain more directly.

But a sandwich called The Squish.

I could picture it.

On a board.

In chalk.

Perhaps with a little star.

I returned the following morning.

“Hello again,” said Rita.

A promising start.

She recognised me.

I ordered tea and toast.

At eleven, I returned for another tea.

At one, I came in for lunch.

At three, Rita saw me through the window and opened the door before I reached it.

“You forget something?”

“Just fancied a drink.”

“You’ve had quite a few.”

“I’m very thirsty locally.”

She stepped aside.

I sat at the same table.

Consistency mattered.

You couldn’t become a regular if the café had to keep finding you.

On the third day, Rita said, “Usual?”

I nearly lost my shape.

“Yes,” I said softly.

“Table?”

“Oh.”

She pointed.

“Your usual table?”

“Yes. That too.”

I sat down.

We hadn’t established a usual order because I was still testing which one made me look most like a person worth putting between bread.

Tea alone felt insufficient.

Cake suggested a lack of focus.

Soup was Dennis’s territory, and I didn’t want an incident.

I settled on a cheese sandwich.

Respectable.

Familiar.

A foundation.

Then I began developing my own.

At home, I wrote down everything I liked.

Cheese.

Roasted peppers.

Pickle.

Crisps.

A little mayonnaise.

A sausage.

One custard cream.

I studied the list.

It seemed generous.

People appreciate generosity.

I took it to Rita.

She read it.

Turned the page over.

There was nothing on the back.

I don’t know what she’d hoped to find.

An explanation, perhaps.

“That’s three lunches.”

“They’d share bread.”

She read it again.

“Why’s there a biscuit?”

“A surprise.”

“It was.”

“You could leave it whole. Or break it up.”

“We could also leave it out.”

I considered this.

“Would that weaken the concept?”

Rita put the paper on the counter.

“Squish, this is something you eat over the sink after a difficult phone call.”

“It’s an early draft.”

“I’d leave it early.”

I took the list back.

Outside, I crossed out the custard cream.

Then I drew an arrow and wrote:

POSSIBLE SIDE.

I wasn’t giving up everything.

A week later, I bought a badge.

Plain white.

A little red border.

I wrote on it:

ASK ME ABOUT THE SQUISH.

I pinned it to my bow tie.

Then I went to the café and sat facing the room.

Nobody asked.

I turned slightly towards a woman eating a jacket potato.

Still nothing.

I angled the badge towards the light.

She leaned over.

My little heart lifted.

“Could you pass the pepper?”

I passed it.

A useful conversation.

We’d established contact.

I made a tabletop sign.

Small.

Tasteful.

THE SQUISH IS COMING.

Rita removed it.

“People think we’re closing.”

“I can change the wording.”

“It’s covering the soup.”

I put it in my bag.

There would be setbacks.

Even Dennis must have started somewhere.

I began talking to customers.

Briefly.

Naturally.

Whenever they appeared to be between bites.

“Can I ask your opinion on a possible new sandwich?”

One woman said, “Sounds nice, love.”

She hadn’t heard the ingredients.

I marked her down as strong support.

A man said he hadn’t read my leaflet.

Open-minded.

Another said, “Please move.”

Wanted progress.

I had brought a notebook to keep track.

On the first page, I wrote:

PUBLIC RESPONSE.

Below that:

GENERALLY POSITIVE.

It was important not to let the handwriting be influenced by a few individuals.

Rita caught me standing beside the till.

“What are you doing?”

“Gauging interest.”

“You’re blocking the card machine.”

“Would another position be better?”

“Your chair.”

I returned to headquarters.

That was what I had begun calling the table.

Only privately.

I hadn’t lost perspective.

Keith came in the following Tuesday.

I noticed him immediately.

He held the door for a woman with a pushchair.

She thanked him.

Rita called, “Morning, Keith.”

“Morning, Rita.”

No effort.

No badge.

He was good.

At the till, Rita gave him an extra pound in change.

He returned it.

I watched the exchange.

That would be remembered.

I found a pound in my bag and wondered whether there was any natural way to return it.

There wasn’t.

I put it away.

A campaign has to have standards.

Keith sat down with his lunch.

Then, before leaving, he stacked his plate on his cup and carried both to the counter.

Rita thanked him.

I looked at my own plate.

I carried it over.

Then my cup.

Then my saucer.

“Thank you,” said Rita.

I spotted a crumb beside the sugar.

Picked it up.

“Leave that.”

I put it down.

Not every initiative would be welcomed.

The next Tuesday, Keith sat near me.

“Nice badge.”

“Thank you.”

“What’s The Squish?”

At last.

I explained.

He listened properly.

That was disarming.

Most people looked for their food halfway through.

“Sounds good,” he said.

“Does it?”

“Peppers and cheese. Hard to go wrong.”

“There were other elements originally.”

He nodded, as though respecting a difficult past.

Then he looked down at his plate.

“I’ve got more than I’ll eat. Want the other half?”

He offered me half his sandwich.

Just like that.

Sharing.

He had brought sharing into it.

I hadn’t budgeted for sharing.

I accepted because refusing would have looked petty.

And because it looked delicious.

I sat there eating half my rival’s lunch while he asked whether I lived nearby.

It was humiliating how pleasant he made it.

At home, I revised the recipe.

No sausage.

No crisps.

No custard cream, even on the side.

Roasted red pepper.

Mozzarella.

A little pickle.

Toasted.

Pressed.

Much better.

I made one.

It tasted good.

I made another.

That tasted good too.

By the third, I was mostly conducting research into whether I could continue eating sandwiches.

The answer was yes, but with less dignity.

I decided to hold a tasting.

At the café.

Rita said no.

I said it would be small.

She said no again, which seemed to address size quite firmly.

I went home.

The following day, I returned with four tiny samples in a box.

I had technically already held the tasting in my kitchen.

I was merely bringing the results to people.

There were feedback cards.

A pencil on a string.

I’d attached it to a small jar because established organisations secure their pencils.

A man asked whether he could take it to his table.

“Of course.”

He picked it up.

The jar followed.

We both pretended that was intended.

My first question was:

HOW PLEASED WOULD YOU BE TO SEE THIS AVAILABLE PERMANENTLY?

Underneath were three boxes.

PLEASED.

VERY PLEASED.

EXTREMELY PLEASED.

A woman read them.

“What if I’m not pleased?”

I turned the card over.

“There’s room on the back.”

My second question was:

WHICH PART REMINDED YOU MOST OF SQUISH?

Someone wrote:

I DON’T KNOW YOU.

That was useful.

A visibility problem.

I underlined it.

Rita came over.

She looked at the samples.

The cards.

The secured pencil.

“Are you taking orders?”

“No.”

“Good.”

“Would you like to try—”

“Did you make these here?”

“At home.”

“Then you can’t hand them round in my café.”

I looked at the remaining samples.

“But they’re free.”

“So’s the argument. Put them away.”

I put them away.

There were practical details to hospitality that I hadn’t anticipated.

The man with the pencil brought it back.

Still attached to the jar.

“Good sandwich,” he said.

I smiled.

Then he added, “Bit wet.”

Keith, who had tried a sample before Rita stopped us, nodded.

“Drain the peppers first.”

I looked at him.

“Sorry?”

“Peppers. Give them a bit longer in the sieve.”

It was an excellent suggestion.

I hated that.

He had provided one sentence and improved several days of work.

That evening, I drained the peppers.

The sandwich was noticeably better.

I wrote at the top of the recipe:

REVISED ORIGINAL.

Then I added a small note at the bottom.

Keith had helped.

I could admit that.

In small writing.

By then, people had begun to recognise me.

“The sandwich fellow.”

I heard a woman say it as I passed.

I kept going.

Calmly.

At a pace that suggested I had been recognisable for years.

The badge was working.

Unfortunately, it wasn’t working in the way I’d hoped.

A man came over while I was drinking tea.

“Can I have a Squish?”

I nearly stood up.

“Absolutely.”

“Toasted?”

“Yes.”

“Brown bread?”

“Certainly.”

“And a coffee.”

I looked towards Rita.

Rita looked towards me.

It was a long distance for a small café.

“He thinks you work here,” she said.

“I’m gathering that.”

The man looked at my badge.

Then my table.

Then the notebook.

“Do you not?”

“Not formally.”

“Squish,” said Rita.

“Not at all.”

He went to the counter.

I drank my tea.

Rita came over.

“The badge has to go.”

“But people are asking.”

“Yes. For things I then have to explain aren’t available.”

“That’s demand.”

“That’s my lunch queue.”

I unpinned it.

Held it for a moment.

“Can I wear it on the way home?”

“You can wear it in the bath. Just take it off in here.”

I put it in my bag.

There was a lot of campaign material in the bag by then.

Not much room for lunch.

I had become a person who could explain his sandwich more easily than produce one.

That should have concerned me.

Instead, I decided it was time to speak to Keith.

Directly.

Politely.

Get everything out in the open.

He was stirring his tea when I approached.

“May I?”

He moved a chair.

I sat.

He offered me the sugar.

“No, thank you.”

A strong start.

I wasn’t accepting everything he handed me.

“Keith, are you hoping to get something named after you?”

He looked surprised.

“What?”

“A sandwich.”

“No.”

“Not even a small baguette?”

“No.”

“A roll?”

He considered me.

“Are you offering?”

“No. I’m asking about your intentions.”

He put down the spoon.

I could see him trying to locate the conversation we were having.

It was difficult.

I had brought it from quite far away.

“You suggested the pickle,” I said.

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Thought it would taste nice.”

I waited.

He waited too.

That appeared to be the whole reason.

I had no prepared response to someone acting for sandwich reasons.

“Rita mentions you,” I said.

“Does she?”

“Yes.”

“Nice.”

He drank his tea.

I tried another approach.

“You come in regularly.”

“Tuesdays.”

“Only Tuesdays?”

“Margaret goes swimming.”

I looked at him.

He looked back.

“I drop her off. Have lunch. Pick her up.”

That was it.

I had reorganised my week around a man waiting for Margaret to finish her lengths.

“Do you swim?” he asked.

“Not at present.”

“She likes it.”

“Good.”

“Bad knee.”

“Sorry.”

“Swimming helps.”

We were discussing Margaret’s knee.

Somehow, he’d brought me right back to being a normal person.

I went home and looked at my notes.

There were three pages about Keith.

One said:

KEITH — GOOD WITH PEOPLE.

Another said:

NO BADGE. WHY?

I closed the notebook.

It was time to speak plainly to Rita.

Unfortunately, I had already prepared remarks.

I’d worked hard on them.

It seemed wasteful not to use at least some.

I arrived the next day just before lunch.

Rita was behind the counter.

I waited until she looked free.

Then a man joined the queue behind me.

Then another.

I should have let them go first.

But I had taken out the papers.

That makes a moment feel official.

“Rita.”

“Brown or white?”

“I’ll address bread shortly.”

She stopped reaching for the loaf.

“I’d first like to thank the café community.”

Behind me, a man shifted his weight.

Nobody had thanked him in a lunch queue before.

“I’ve been made very welcome.”

Rita folded her arms.

“And over the past few weeks, I believe we’ve seen considerable interest in—”

“Squish. What do you want?”

“A place on the board.”

She looked at the specials board.

Then at me.

“You could have asked me that without all the paper.”

“I wanted to explain the journey.”

“Three people behind you would like to finish theirs.”

I looked round.

There were five now.

One had taken off his coat.

He was settling in.

“I’m sorry,” I said.

I gathered the papers.

They slipped.

All over the floor.

It was quite a lot of journey.

I bent down.

The man behind me bent down too.

Keith appeared from his table.

Of course he did.

He began collecting pages.

Quietly.

Helpfully.

Without a badge announcing it.

Then he stopped.

I knew which page he had before he turned it round.

WHY KEITH?

WHY NOW?

Large letters.

Underlined.

He looked at me.

I looked at the page.

Then at him.

“That requires context.”

“Probably does.”

“It’s not the main part.”

He glanced at the little number in the corner.

Page two.

I held out my hand.

He gave it back.

Then he collected page three without reading it.

I appreciated him more in that moment than during any of the sharing.

Rita pointed towards my table.

“Sit.”

I sat.

Keith sat too.

This was generous of him, considering he had just discovered he featured in a document.

I put the papers in my bag.

Then, after a moment, I took out the recipe.

Only the recipe.

“I do think it’s good,” I said.

Keith nodded.

“It is.”

“I drained the peppers.”

“Thought you might.”

I looked at him.

“Thank you.”

“You’re welcome.”

We sat for a while.

The queue moved.

People got their lunches.

The café continued to function despite having received only part of my speech.

Eventually, Rita came over.

“Show me.”

I gave her the recipe.

She read it.

“No biscuit?”

“No biscuit.”

“Right.”

She went into the kitchen.

I watched the doorway.

Keith drank his tea.

“How long does Margaret swim for?” I asked.

“Forty minutes.”

I nodded.

I would have committed to several years.

A few minutes later, Rita returned with a toasted sandwich.

My sandwich.

She’d made it properly.

The mozzarella had melted.

The bread was golden.

The peppers were not leaking onto anything.

She cut it.

Took a bite.

I leaned forward.

“The balance of—”

She raised one finger.

I stopped.

She chewed.

It was the longest period of useful silence I’d contributed to the campaign.

Then she took another bite.

My little heart became difficult to house.

“Well?” I asked.

“It’s good.”

I tried to respond normally.

“Thank you.”

It came out very small.

Rita looked at the recipe again.

“Could do it as a special.”

I stayed still.

I didn’t want to shake any of the words loose.

“Really?”

“Really.”

“And would it—”

“Yes, Squish. We’ll call it The Squish.”

I had prepared for this.

A gracious response.

A little nod.

Something like, “I think people will enjoy it.”

What I actually said was, “Oh!”

Then I said it again.

Rita waited.

Keith smiled.

I looked down at the sandwich.

It was going to have my name.

A proper thing.

Something someone might choose because they were hungry, rather than because I’d approached them with a pencil.

Rita put the plate down.

“Couple of conditions.”

I straightened.

“The badge stays away.”

“Yes.”

“No more interviewing people.”

“Understood.”

“And you’re paying for that one.”

“Of course.”

“And Keith’s tea.”

I looked at Keith.

He began to object.

“No,” I said. “That’s fair.”

Then Rita looked at the recipe once more.

“Whose idea was the pickle?”

I could have said mine.

The page had my name on it.

Keith wouldn’t have corrected me.

That made it worse.

“Keith’s,” I said.

Rita nodded.

“Thought so.”

He picked up his cup.

I decided not to compete with a man who could win things accidentally.

The following Tuesday, I arrived before Keith.

Before most people.

Rita was writing on the board.

I waited outside until she finished.

I didn’t want to watch her spell me.

That felt private.

Then I went in.

There it was.

THE SQUISH.

Roasted red pepper, mozzarella & pickle.

Toasted.

My name.

In chalk.

Underneath, the soup was leek and potato.

I felt very good about our position.

“Morning,” said Rita.

“Morning.”

“Usual?”

I looked at the board.

Then at her.

This time, we both knew what she meant.

“Yes, please.”

I sat at my table.

The table had never been officially designated headquarters.

That was probably for the best.

I took out my notebook.

Opened it to a clean page.

Wrote:

ON THE BOARD.

Then I stopped.

Nothing else needed doing.

That was a strange feeling.

I had become used to carrying a plan.

Without one, my bag seemed enormous.

A woman came in.

Looked at the board.

“I’ll try The Squish.”

I squeezed my teacup.

Tea rose over the rim.

I put it down very carefully.

Rita glanced at me.

“Cloth?”

“Yes, please.”

The woman turned.

“Did you spill?”

“A little.”

“Easy done.”

I nodded.

There was no reason to explain that she had just ordered my life’s work.

Rita brought my sandwich.

Hot.

Golden.

Cut diagonally.

I had never imagined myself diagonal before.

It suited me.

Keith arrived while I was admiring it.

“Made it, then.”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

He sat opposite.

He didn’t say anything about page two.

A generous man.

Rita put his tea down.

“Want your usual?”

“Yes, please.”

“And a Squish?”

He looked at me.

“Think I’d better.”

She went away.

I picked up my sandwich.

Took a bite.

Lovely bread.

Warm cheese.

Sweet peppers.

Then the pickle.

I paused.

Chewed.

Looked towards the counter.

Looked at the board.

Looked at Keith.

There had been a great deal of campaigning.

I hadn’t wanted to complicate it by mentioning that I preferred the earlier version.

Without the pickle.

For several seconds, I considered keeping that information inside me.

Along with the pickle.

Then I raised a little hand.

“Rita?”

She looked over.

“Would it be terribly difficult to have mine without—”

Her eyes moved to the board.

Then back to me.

Keith stopped stirring.

I could hear the spoon settle against the cup.

Outside, a bus went past.

People were carrying on with lives they hadn’t turned into this.

“Without what?” Rita asked.

I looked at the sandwich.

There were things a person could still choose not to become.

“Nothing,” I said.

I took another bite.

“Lovely.”

Keith reached for the sugar.

I ate the pickle.

All of it.

There would be no amendment.

Not while I was in office.
