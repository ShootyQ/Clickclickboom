# The Mystery of the Moving Garden Chair

I once investigated a crime in my garden.

I say crime.

Nothing had been taken.

Nobody had been hurt.

The police were quite firm about both those points.

But my chair was moving.

Three inches to the left.

Not enough for a normal person to care.

Unfortunately, it was my chair.

I had spent most of a summer finding its proper position.

There was a place beside the kitchen window where the sun warmed my front, the fence sheltered my back, and I could reach the biscuit tin without getting up.

You cannot buy an arrangement like that.

You have to earn it through several disappointing afternoons.

An inch too far forward and the sun went in my eyes.

An inch too far back and Mrs Bell from next door could see me over the fence.

Mrs Bell is lovely.

But if she can see you, she assumes you’re available for a conversation about her foot.

The foot is improving.

It has been improving for four years.

I had found a spot where I could enjoy my tea, reach a biscuit, and remain uninformed about it.

Then, on a Wednesday, I sat down and missed the windowsill.

Only just.

My little hand brushed the edge.

I tried again.

Slowly, in case I’d become worse at reaching.

Still short.

I looked at my arm.

It looked like my usual arm.

No obvious recent losses.

I looked at the window.

The house seemed to be where I’d left it.

Then I noticed the chair.

It was slightly to the left.

I moved it back.

That should have been the story.

A chair was in the wrong place, so I put it in the right place.

You could tell it to someone waiting for a lift.

They wouldn’t enjoy it, but it would be over before the lift arrived.

The next afternoon, it happened again.

I came out with my tea.

Sat down.

Reached for the tin.

Missed.

I looked at the chair.

There was its old mark on the paving.

And there was its leg.

Some distance from its responsibilities.

I stood up.

Walked round it.

Looked underneath.

Nothing.

I don’t know what I’d expected to find underneath.

A man, perhaps.

A very flat man with a hobby.

I moved the chair back.

Then I stood in the kitchen window watching it.

After ten minutes, I felt ridiculous.

After twenty, I felt committed.

I stayed for forty.

The chair did nothing.

I began to resent how innocent it looked.

The following morning, I put a chalk mark beside each leg.

Small marks.

Discreet.

I wasn’t being strange about it.

Then I put a larger mark beside the back left leg, because I couldn’t see the discreet one from the window.

Then I drew a line between them.

By lunchtime, the chair appeared to have been involved in a parking accident.

Mrs Bell called over the fence.

“Everything all right?”

“Yes.”

“What are the marks?”

“Just keeping track of something.”

“What sort of something?”

I considered how to answer without encouraging further questions.

“Chair.”

That did not achieve it.

She came round.

Mrs Bell can come round very quickly for someone with that foot.

I explained what had happened.

She listened.

Looked at the chair.

Looked at the window.

“Wind,” she said.

“It’s a fairly heavy chair.”

“Wind can be heavy.”

I thought about that.

It sounded wrong, but not in a way I could immediately prosecute.

“Does anything else move?” she asked.

“No.”

“Anything missing?”

“No.”

“Windows open at night?”

“No.”

She looked faintly disappointed.

I felt I’d brought her a poor-quality incident.

“Sorry,” I said.

“No, no. It’s your garden.”

She examined the chalk.

“Could be foxes.”

“Moving a chair?”

“They’re very bold now.”

I pictured a fox looking over his shoulder while another fox took the other end.

“Would they put it back down?”

“Depends what they wanted.”

I wasn’t sure how much longer I could manage the conversation.

She went home.

I watched her go.

She was walking well.

I decided not to mention it.

That afternoon, the chair moved again.

Precisely how far, I couldn’t say.

Which bothered me.

If you’re going to be upset about something, you ought to know the size of it.

I went next door and borrowed a tape measure.

Mrs Bell gave me one from a drawer.

“What are you measuring?”

“The movement.”

She handed it over more slowly.

It was a metal tape.

I had never used one before.

I pulled out a little length.

Read it.

Pressed the button.

The tape shot back into its case with a noise like a snake being told to get in the car.

I dropped it.

Mrs Bell watched from her doorstep.

“Mind your fingers.”

I checked.

Still the usual number.

After that, I fed it back in by hand.

It took a while, but we parted on better terms.

The chair had moved three inches.

Exactly.

I measured it twice.

Then I measured from the fence.

Then the drain.

Then the large flowerpot.

The flowerpot gave a different answer.

For several minutes, I thought the garden might be wider than it had been that morning.

Then I remembered moving the flowerpot to get the tape measure behind it.

I put it back.

Measured again.

It was worse.

I no longer knew where back was.

I made a note to stop using the flowerpot as a witness.

That evening, I bought a notebook.

A proper one.

Blue cover.

Spiral binding.

I wrote on the first page:

CHAIR MOVEMENT.

Then, underneath:

WEDNESDAY: LEFT.

THURSDAY: LEFT.

FRIDAY: ALSO LEFT.

It was a strong opening.

On the next page, I drew the garden.

The chair was easy.

The house took longer because I accidentally made the kitchen too small, then tried to correct it without starting again.

By the time I’d finished, the drawing suggested my house was gradually swallowing the patio.

I labelled the flowerpot:

NOT RELIABLE.

Then I began a list of people with access.

Me.

Mrs Bell.

Mr Finch, who had once retrieved a football.

The man who cleaned the gutters.

I crossed him out.

He hadn’t been for eight months.

Then I put him back.

It was a spiral notebook.

There was room to be fair.

I decided to make enquiries.

Casual enquiries.

I didn’t want the whole street thinking I’d gone peculiar over a chair.

I found Mr Finch clipping his hedge.

“Afternoon.”

“Afternoon.”

“Lovely weather.”

“Certainly is.”

A promising start.

We were two ordinary neighbours enjoying an ordinary exchange.

“Where were you between lunch and the chair?”

His shears stopped.

I heard what I’d said.

Neither of us seemed equipped to repair it.

“Sorry,” I said. “I mean yesterday.”

“Yesterday?”

“Yes.”

“When?”

“Between lunch and—”

I stopped.

Mr Finch lowered the shears.

“Has something happened?”

“Nothing serious.”

“What sort of nothing?”

“My chair’s been moved.”

“Have you asked your chair?”

He laughed.

I laughed too.

A little.

I wanted to keep him talking.

“I just wondered whether you’d seen anyone.”

“In your garden?”

“Yes.”

“No.”

He considered me.

“You been in mine?”

“No.”

“Right.”

Our relationship had become less casual.

At number twelve, a woman called Denise answered the door.

I asked whether she’d noticed anything unusual.

She went very still.

Then she said, “Is this about the parcel?”

I hadn’t known there was a parcel.

Now I had a lead.

“What parcel?”

“The one I took in.”

“When?”

“November.”

“What was in it?”

“I didn’t open it.”

She said this quite quickly.

I looked down at the notebook.

I wasn’t sure how to write that without making her look worse.

“I’m asking about a chair,” I said.

“Oh, thank goodness.”

The relief was enormous.

I decided to leave the parcel where it was.

Whatever that meant.

At number fourteen, Mr Dobbs was standing beside his lawn mower.

He was an elderly man with a thick cardigan and the expression of someone who had already tightened everything he intended to tighten.

“I wondered whether you’d heard any scraping,” I said.

He looked up sharply.

“It’s started again.”

My little arm went cold.

“What has?”

He pointed at the mower.

“Listen.”

It made a clank.

Then a cough.

Then something fell off.

I waited while he examined the piece.

“Would that move a chair?” I asked.

He looked at me.

“Depends how hard you threw it.”

I went home with three pages of notes and considerably fewer people likely to lend me things.

It was time for evidence.

I spread flour round the chair.

A thin, even layer.

I’d seen this done.

Admittedly, it had been around a cake tin, but the principle seemed sound.

Anything crossing the flour would leave a mark.

I went to bed feeling clever.

This is often the stage immediately before a problem.

At two in the morning, I woke up.

I wondered whether the flour was still there.

Perhaps it had blown away.

Perhaps something was happening at that very moment.

I went downstairs.

Out into the garden.

Everything was quiet.

The chair stood in the moonlight.

The flour looked excellent.

I checked the far side.

Then the back.

Then I went in.

At seven, I returned with my notebook.

There was a trail.

A broad, unmistakable trail through the flour.

Reddish in places.

I crouched beside it.

Touched it.

Examined my little hand.

“Oh,” I said.

Then I followed it.

It went round the chair.

Across the patio.

Over the doorstep.

Into the kitchen.

And ended precisely where I’d stood deciding whether to have a piece of toast at two in the morning.

I stood at the end of my own trail.

For a long time.

It is difficult to feel like an investigator when the evidence has come home and gone to bed with you.

Mrs Bell appeared at the fence.

“Any luck?”

“I’ve established someone was in the garden.”

“When?”

“About two.”

“Did you see them?”

I closed the notebook.

“Not from the front.”

She came round.

She looked at the flour.

Then at me.

“Is that you?”

“It’s consistent with me.”

“Oh, love.”

I had been hoping not to reach oh, love.

It is a very difficult place to conduct an investigation from.

By Sunday, I had changed tactics.

I would watch the chair in person.

Continuously.

No flour.

No measuring.

Just patient observation.

I positioned myself behind the shrub beside the shed.

It was a small shrub.

Smaller than me.

From the house, it looked as though someone had placed a little red pudding behind a sprig of parsley.

But it was the best cover available.

I settled in.

Opened the notebook.

And waited.

Five minutes later, Mrs Bell called over.

“Are you hiding?”

“No.”

“You look as though you’re hiding.”

“I’m observing.”

“Would you like tea?”

I hesitated.

The observer should not accept refreshments at a sensitive stage.

On the other hand, she was offering.

“Yes, please.”

She brought tea.

And a folding chair.

“You’ll be uncomfortable down there.”

I looked at her chair.

Then at mine.

The garden was beginning to contain too many chairs for my liking.

She sat beside the shrub.

“Go on, then.”

“Go on what?”

“Observe.”

I tried.

Mrs Bell drank her tea.

“How’s the investigation?”

“Quietly.”

A pause.

Then she said, “I’ve been meaning to tell you about my foot.”

Mr Finch came round twenty minutes later.

He said he was checking whether I wanted him to keep an eye out.

Then he sat on an upturned plant pot.

Not the marked one.

I insisted.

Denise arrived with biscuits.

I didn’t ask whether they had come in a parcel.

Mr Dobbs brought a camping stool and half a pork pie wrapped in foil.

“I’ve got an hour,” he said.

Nobody had invited him.

He settled down as though we were about to watch somebody fail a driving test.

For a while, we all looked at my chair.

It had never had so much attention.

“Anything happened?” Mr Finch asked.

“No.”

“How long’s it usually take?”

“It isn’t a performance.”

“Right.”

He ate a biscuit.

We continued watching.

A fly landed on the chair.

Everyone leaned forward.

The fly left.

Everyone leaned back.

I wrote nothing.

There are moments when writing something down would make it worse.

After half an hour, Mr Dobbs said, “Could somebody be getting underneath?”

“I’ve checked.”

“What about under the patio?”

I looked at him.

“Through the paving?”

“Things settle.”

“Not usually and then take a seat.”

Mrs Bell leaned towards Denise.

“He’s tired.”

I was three feet away.

I could hear her perfectly.

But I was also behind a shrub with a notebook, so I wasn’t in a strong position to argue.

Eventually, they went home.

They had eaten all the biscuits.

The chair hadn’t moved.

I sat in it.

Reached through the window.

Found the empty tin.

And had a very long think about my neighbours.

Later, I checked the chalk.

Three inches left.

Again.

I stood there holding the empty tin.

Whatever was doing it had waited until they’d gone.

I almost admired that.

On Monday, I began reconstructing my afternoons.

Not the whole afternoon.

Only the relevant parts.

I wrote down when I opened the kitchen window.

When I put out the tea.

When I sat down.

When I noticed the chair.

I discovered the movement always happened sometime after I’d settled in.

This was important.

I underlined it.

Then I underlined it again, a little farther down, because the first line wasn’t straight.

I also found a shiny mark on the left arm of the chair.

A faint smear.

It looked reddish.

I touched it.

Then looked at myself.

There was rather a lot of me available as a comparison.

I considered this.

Then I wrote:

COULD BE PAINT.

The chair was green.

I put a question mark after paint.

That afternoon, I heard a scrape.

Only a small one.

I was reaching through the window at the time.

I stopped.

Listened.

Nothing.

I looked behind the chair.

Nothing.

I looked under it.

Still nothing.

By then, I knew more about the underside of that chair than most people know about their grandparents.

I checked the chalk.

Three inches.

My heart gave a little jump.

I had been there when it happened.

Present at the scene.

Within arm’s reach.

Whoever was responsible was becoming extremely bold.

I gathered everyone the following afternoon.

I didn’t call it a gathering.

I said I had found something and they might like to see.

Mr Dobbs brought his stool immediately.

Mrs Bell asked whether she should bring a plate.

“No,” I said.

She brought one anyway.

I propped my diagram against the fence.

Mr Finch examined it.

“What’s this?”

“The kitchen.”

“Why’s it shaped like a boot?”

“I revised it.”

He pointed.

“And this?”

“Flowerpot.”

“Why does it say not reliable?”

“Because we’re not using it.”

There was a pause.

Then Denise said, “Have you thought about moving the chair somewhere else?”

“Yes,” I said. “Someone’s been very helpful with that.”

I stood beside the chair.

“Now. This appears to happen during the afternoon.”

They nodded.

“I’ve ruled out the wind.”

Mrs Bell pursed her lips.

I hadn’t technically ruled it out.

But I was speaking.

“And there is a scraping sound.”

Mr Dobbs nodded firmly.

He liked a scraping sound.

“So I’m going to repeat exactly what I normally do.”

Mr Finch folded his arms.

I opened the kitchen window.

Put my tea on the little table.

Placed the biscuit tin on the windowsill.

Sat down.

“Ordinarily,” I said, “I’ll sit here for a while.”

Nobody moved.

“I won’t do the whole while.”

“Appreciated,” said Mr Finch.

“Then I reach for a biscuit.”

I stretched my little arm through the window.

A little farther.

My hand landed beside the tin.

I leaned.

My fingers stuck lightly to the sill.

I pulled myself back.

My side pressed against the chair.

The chair scraped left.

Exactly three inches.

Nobody said anything.

I was holding a biscuit.

I looked at it.

Then at the chalk.

Then at the biscuit again.

It had no useful advice.

Mrs Bell leaned forward.

“Do that again.”

I swallowed.

“Which bit?”

“The bit where you moved the chair.”

“I’m not sure we should jump to—”

“Do it again, love.”

I put the biscuit down.

There was no reason to.

I just needed something to do that wasn’t the demonstration.

Then I reached.

Stuck.

Pulled.

Scrape.

Mr Finch looked away.

His mouth had become very small.

Denise put a hand over hers.

Mr Dobbs unfolded the tape measure.

I hadn’t asked him to.

He measured the distance between the chalk and the leg.

“Three inches.”

“Yes,” I said.

“Exactly.”

“Yes.”

“Remarkably consistent.”

“Thank you.”

I don’t know why I thanked him.

There should be a separate word for when someone compliments the precision of your humiliation.

Mr Finch made a noise.

Mrs Bell looked at him.

He shook his head.

She looked back at me.

“So you’ve been doing it yourself.”

“It would appear I’ve been present.”

Mr Finch had to turn round.

His shoulders moved.

Denise bent over the plate.

I looked at my diagram.

The red smear.

The flour.

The list of suspects.

ME had been at the top.

I had written it down on the first day.

Then spent the rest of the week being generous enough not to pursue it.

“What about the mornings?” I asked.

I wasn’t arguing.

I was hoping.

“You said afternoons,” Mrs Bell said.

“Yes, but—”

“And every morning you put it back.”

I looked at her.

She looked at me.

It was horrible how little she needed the notebook.

Mr Dobbs straightened up.

“Could you do that with a chest of drawers?”

I turned.

“What?”

“I’ve got one needs shifting in the spare room.”

“This isn’t a service.”

“Only a few inches.”

“It depends on the window.”

“What?”

“And I’d need a biscuit.”

He considered it.

“Could arrange something.”

Mr Finch had started laughing properly now.

Denise was trying to stop.

That was worse.

She kept managing half a serious face before losing it again.

Mrs Bell patted my arm.

“Well. At least you’ve solved it.”

I looked at the chair.

“I was hoping to catch someone I could be annoyed with.”

“Oh, you have.”

That finished Mr Finch.

He sat down on the plant pot.

The unreliable one.

I let him.

There was no point protecting its reputation now.

I put away the diagram.

Closed the notebook.

Collected the tea things.

Everyone stayed.

Apparently, we were having a lovely afternoon.

Mr Dobbs asked whether I had any more biscuits.

I brought the tin outside and put it on the table.

It was an astonishingly simple thing to do.

I didn’t enjoy discovering it.

For the next hour, every time I reached for anything, somebody watched.

If I picked up my cup, Mr Finch put a hand against his chair.

If I leaned towards the plate, Denise said, “Steady.”

At one point, Mrs Bell dropped her teaspoon.

I went to pick it up.

Mr Dobbs said, “Mind the house.”

I sat back down.

He could get it himself.

Eventually, they left.

Mrs Bell stayed behind to help.

She rubbed out the chalk marks.

I put the tape measure in its case.

Slowly.

We had already had our difficulties, and I wasn’t letting it get the last word.

“Don’t worry,” she said. “We’ve all done silly things.”

“Have we?”

“Oh, yes.”

I waited.

She rinsed a cup.

“Any examples?”

“Nothing comes to mind.”

I looked at her.

She looked into the cup.

I knew what she was doing.

Apparently, innocence was something you could choose not to explain.

The next afternoon, I moved the biscuit tin.

Closer to the chair.

Within easy reach.

Then I sat down.

Tea.

Sunshine.

A whole biscuit retrieved without altering the layout of the garden.

Wonderful.

I remained there for an hour.

Nothing scraped.

Nothing shifted.

At last, I went inside.

Made another cup of tea.

Came back out.

The chair had moved.

Three inches.

To the right.

I stood very still.

Then I looked at the fence.

Nobody visible.

I looked at the chair.

Then the window.

Then the biscuit tin.

From next door, Mrs Bell called, “Have you checked the other arm?”

I closed my eyes.

Somewhere farther along, Mr Finch started laughing.

There are people who can be trusted with an explanation.

I had gathered none of them.

I went inside and shut the window.

A moment later, Mrs Bell called again.

“Don’t forget your biscuits.”

I opened it.

Reached out.

Scrape.

“Oh, for goodness’ sake,” I said.

I left the chair where it was.

It had clearly found a better position for itself.
